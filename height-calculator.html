What this calculator does

This tool estimates a child's adult height — how tall they're likely to be once they've finished growing — based on how tall their biological mother and father are. It uses the mid-parental height method, the same quick estimate pediatricians often use as a starting point. It is not a measurement of a child's current height, and it is an estimate, not a prediction you can bank on.

The mid-parental height method

The method is simple. Add the two parents' heights, adjust for the child's sex, and divide by two:

  • Boys: (mother's height + father's height + 13 cm) ÷ 2
  • Girls: (mother's height + father's height − 13 cm) ÷ 2

The 13 cm (about 5 inches) is the average height difference between adult men and women. For example, a boy with a 162 cm mother and a 178 cm father: (162 + 178 + 13) ÷ 2 = 176.5 cm (about 5 ft 9 in). Because real outcomes vary, this calculator also shows a range of roughly 8.5 cm (3.3 inches) either side, which is where most children in that situation land.

How accurate is it?

Genetics account for about 80% of height, so a parent-based estimate is a sensible ballpark — but the remaining 20% (nutrition, sleep, general health, hormones, and illness during growth years) can easily move the final figure by several centimetres. That's why the range matters more than the single number. If a child's growth is tracking far outside the expected range on a pediatric growth chart, that's a conversation for a doctor, not a calculator.

Other ways height is predicted

  • Bone-age X-ray (Bayley-Pinneau method): a hand X-ray shows how much growth plate remains, giving doctors a more individualised prediction than parent height alone.
  • Growth-chart tracking: plotting a child's height percentile over time. Children tend to follow their own curve, so a consistent percentile is often projected forward.
  • The "double it at age two" rule of thumb: a rough folk estimate that doubles a child's height at age two. It's crude and less reliable than the mid-parental method.

What affects a child's final height

  • Genetics — the largest single factor, spread across many genes, not just the parents.
  • Nutrition — adequate protein, calcium, and overall calories during growth years.
  • Sleep and general health — growth hormone is released mainly during deep sleep; chronic illness can slow growth.
  • Puberty timing — earlier or later puberty shifts when growth plates close.

Sources & method

This calculator uses the mid-parental height formula described above, a long-standing estimate used in pediatric practice. Average-height reference figures are drawn from national health surveys (for example the CDC in the US and the NHS/ONS in the UK). Growth prediction is inherently uncertain; for any concern about a child's growth, consult a pediatrician, who can use growth charts and, if needed, a bone-age assessment. This tool is for general information and is not medical advice.
