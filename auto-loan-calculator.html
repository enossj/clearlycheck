How to Use This Auto Loan Calculator

Start with the vehicle price, then enter any cash incentive, down payment, trade-in value, amount still owed on your trade-in, sales tax, fees, interest rate, and loan term. The calculator estimates how much you would finance, your required monthly payment, total interest, and the amount paid over the loan.

The most useful part is not the monthly payment by itself. Try changing the loan term, interest rate, or down payment and compare the results. A payment that looks affordable can still lead to a much higher financing cost when the loan is stretched over more years.

Tip: When comparing auto loans, look at the amount financed, APR or interest rate, loan term, monthly payment, and total interest together. Focusing only on the monthly payment can hide the cost of a longer loan.

How Is a Car Loan Payment Calculated?

For a standard fixed-rate installment loan, the required payment is based on the amount financed, monthly interest rate, and number of payments. Each payment contains both interest and principal. Early in the schedule, the balance is larger, so the interest portion is larger. As the balance falls, more of each payment goes toward principal.

Payment = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1)

Here, P is the amount financed, r is the monthly interest rate, and n is the number of monthly payments. At 0% interest, the financed amount is simply divided across the number of payments.

What Goes Into the Amount You Finance?

Your vehicle price is only one part of the calculation. This calculator also accounts for incentives, down payment, trade-in equity, sales tax, and other fees. If you have negative equity on your current vehicle, that difference can increase the new amount financed.

Tax rules and fees vary by location and lender, so treat the result as an estimate. Your purchase contract and lender disclosures contain the final numbers.

How Does a Longer Auto Loan Change the Cost?

A longer term spreads the balance across more payments. That can reduce the required monthly payment, but it usually means paying interest for a longer period. Use the term comparison above to see the trade-off for your own numbers.

Long terms can also make it harder to build equity quickly, especially when the vehicle loses value faster than the loan balance falls. This matters if you plan to trade or sell the vehicle before the loan is paid off.

Does a Larger Down Payment Save Money?

A larger down payment reduces the amount you borrow. With the same interest rate and term, borrowing less generally means a lower required payment and less total interest. It also changes how much cash you have left after the purchase, so there is no single down payment that is right for every buyer.

How Does a Trade-In Affect a Car Loan?

Your trade-in creates positive equity when the vehicle is worth more than the amount you still owe. That equity can reduce the amount needed for the next purchase. If you owe more than the vehicle is worth, you have negative equity. Rolling that difference into a new loan increases the amount you borrow and can increase interest costs.

Check your current lender's payoff amount before using an estimated balance. The payoff amount can differ from the balance shown on a recent statement.

Why Taxes and Fees Matter

Sales tax, registration, title charges, documentation fees, and other purchase costs can make the amount you need to pay higher than the advertised vehicle price. Entering those costs gives you a more complete financing estimate.

How Much Can Extra Payments Save?

Extra payments can reduce principal faster. That can shorten the payoff period and reduce future interest. Enter an optional extra payment above to see an estimate based on the current loan.

Check your loan agreement for how additional payments are handled. Some lenders have specific instructions for applying extra money to principal.

How to Compare Two Auto Loan Offers

When two lenders quote different loans, compare more than the monthly payment. Check the amount financed, interest rate or APR, term, required payment, fees, and total amount paid. A lower monthly payment is not necessarily a lower-cost loan if it comes with a longer term or higher financing cost.

APR vs. Interest Rate

The interest rate describes the rate charged on the borrowed balance. APR is a broader measure of borrowing cost and can include certain fees. When a lender provides an APR, read the disclosure to understand what is included and compare offers using the same type of figure.

Dealer Financing vs. Bank or Credit Union Financing

Dealer financing can be convenient because the dealership arranges the loan as part of the purchase. You can also compare a pre-approval or quote from a bank or credit union. Having another offer gives you a reference point when reviewing the dealer's financing terms.

Compare the actual rate, term, fees, payment, and total financing cost rather than assuming one type of lender is always cheaper.

What Your Calculator Result Does Not Include

This calculator estimates the financing side of the purchase. Your real cost of owning the vehicle can also include insurance, fuel, maintenance, repairs, registration renewals, parking, and other transportation expenses. Those costs are separate from the loan payment.

Sources & Methodology

ClearlyCheck uses a standard fixed-rate amortization calculation for the loan payment and schedule. The estimate incorporates the purchase price, incentives, down payment, trade-in equity, sales tax, fees, interest rate, and term entered by the user. The optional extra-payment estimate applies the additional amount to the loan balance each month and estimates the resulting payoff time and interest savings.

Actual loan terms can differ because lenders may use different payment timing, fees, APR calculations, tax treatment, or contract conditions. Verify final figures in the lender's disclosures and purchase documents.
