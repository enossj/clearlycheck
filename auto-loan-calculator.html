How this auto loan calculator works

Estimating a car payment starts with figuring out how much you're actually financing. This calculator adds sales tax to the vehicle price, then subtracts your down payment and trade-in value to get the amount financed. That amount is spread across equal monthly payments using the standard loan amortization formula, which factors in your annual percentage rate (APR) and loan term. Because the formula compounds interest on your remaining balance each month, a higher APR or a longer term will always increase how much interest you pay over the life of the loan, even when it lowers your monthly payment.

APR vs. interest rate: what's the difference

The interest rate is the base cost of borrowing, applied to your remaining loan balance each month. APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is meant to reflect the fuller cost of the loan, including certain finance charges the lender adds, expressed as a yearly rate. On many auto loans the two numbers are close or identical, since add-on fees are less common than on mortgages, but they aren't guaranteed to match. When you're comparing offers from different lenders or dealers, comparing APR rather than the advertised interest rate gives you a more accurate picture of what the loan will actually cost.

How your down payment and trade-in affect your loan

Both a down payment and a trade-in value are subtracted directly from the amount you finance before interest is calculated, so every dollar you put down or trade in lowers your loan balance dollar-for-dollar. A larger down payment or trade-in reduces both your monthly payment and the total interest charged over the loan term, because interest is always calculated on a smaller starting balance. Putting down at least 10–20% is also a common way to avoid being "upside down" — owing more than the car is worth — in the first year or two of ownership, since new vehicles depreciate quickly.

Sources & method

Monthly payments use the standard fixed-rate loan amortization formula. This calculator applies sales tax to the full vehicle price; some states offer a trade-in tax credit that reduces the taxable amount, but since credit rules vary by state, that adjustment isn't included here — check your state's DMV or revenue agency for the exact rule that applies to you.
