The four percentage calculations, explained

Most real-world percentage problems fall into one of four types. Knowing which one you're solving makes the formula obvious:

QuestionFormulaExample
What is X% of Y?(X ÷ 100) × Y20% of 150 = 30
X is what % of Y?(X ÷ Y) × 10030 is 20% of 150
Percentage change from X to Y((Y − X) ÷ X) × 100150 to 180 = +20%
Increase/decrease X by P%X × (1 ± P ÷ 100)150 increased by 20% = 180

Percentage points vs. percentage change

These two get mixed up constantly, especially in news about interest rates, approval ratings, or statistics. If a rate moves from 5% to 8%, that's a 3 percentage point increase (a simple subtraction: 8 − 5), but it's a 60% relative increase (since 3 is 60% of the original 5). Both are correct — they're just answering different questions. Percentage points describe the raw gap; percentage change describes it relative to the starting value.

Why "increase then decrease" doesn't return you to the start

A common source of confusion: if you increase 100 by 10% you get 110, but decreasing 110 by 10% gives you 99, not 100. That's because each percentage is calculated on a different base — the increase was 10% of 100, but the decrease was 10% of 110, a bigger number. This asymmetry is why compounding discounts, sequential price changes, or repeated interest adjustments rarely cancel out the way intuition suggests.

Common everyday uses

  • Discounts: A $150 jacket 20% off costs $150 × (1 − 0.20) = $120.
  • Tips: An 18% tip on a $60 bill is $60 × 0.18 = $10.80.
  • Grade or score comparison: Scoring 42 out of 50 is (42 ÷ 50) × 100 = 84%.
  • Growth tracking: Revenue going from $80,000 to $92,000 is a ((92,000 − 80,000) ÷ 80,000) × 100 = 15% increase.
