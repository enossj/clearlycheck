Why are food labels so confusing?

Food manufacturers are required by law to list every ingredient — but they are not required to explain what those ingredients are in plain language. The result is labels packed with chemical names, E numbers, and technical terms that most people cannot decipher while standing in a supermarket aisle.

This tool translates any ingredient into plain English, covering what it actually is, where it comes from (natural, synthetic, or animal-derived), what it does in the food, and whether there are any known concerns about consuming it.

What are E numbers?

E numbers are a European classification system for food additives. Every approved food additive in the EU gets an "E" prefix followed by a number. They cover preservatives (E200s), colourings (E100s), antioxidants (E300s), emulsifiers (E400s), and many other categories.

The key thing to understand: an E number does not automatically mean dangerous. Vitamin C is E300. Curcumin (from turmeric) is E100. Many E numbers are completely natural and harmless. Others have genuine concerns. You need to look at each one individually — which is exactly what this tool does.

Common ingredients people look up

  • Carrageenan — natural seaweed extract used as a thickener. Controversial due to potential gut inflammation concerns.
  • Xanthan gum — fermentation-derived thickener. Generally considered safe, common in gluten-free products.
  • Maltodextrin — highly processed starch derivative used as a filler. High glycaemic index, minimal nutritional value.
  • Sodium benzoate — preservative that can form benzene (a carcinogen) when combined with vitamin C. Used in acidic drinks.
  • E621 (MSG) — monosodium glutamate, a flavour enhancer. Long considered controversial, but widely accepted as safe by regulators.
  • Soy lecithin — emulsifier derived from soybean oil. Generally safe; relevant for those with soy allergies.

Should I avoid artificial ingredients?

Not necessarily. "Natural" does not automatically mean safe, and "artificial" does not automatically mean harmful. Many synthetic additives are rigorously tested and well understood. Some natural ones have genuine risks at high doses. The key is knowing what each ingredient actually is — which is exactly what this tool helps you do.

Frequently Asked Questions

Not all E numbers are dangerous. E numbers are simply the European food additive numbering system — they cover everything from vitamin C (E300) to natural colours and preservatives. Many are completely safe; some have concerns at high doses. This tool explains each one individually so you can make informed choices.
Carrageenan is a natural thickening agent extracted from red seaweed. It is widely used in dairy products, plant milks, deli meats and infant formula. While approved as safe by most food regulatory bodies, some research has linked high doses to gut inflammation. It is considered controversial — some health-conscious consumers choose to avoid it.
In the US, "natural flavour" means the flavouring was derived from a plant or animal source — but it can still be heavily processed and bear little resemblance to the original ingredient. It is a broad category that manufacturers use to avoid listing specific flavouring compounds. It is not necessarily healthier than "artificial flavour."
MSG (monosodium glutamate, E621) has been studied extensively and is considered safe by the FDA, WHO, and most global food regulators. The idea that MSG causes headaches (sometimes called "Chinese Restaurant Syndrome") has not been consistently supported by controlled research. It naturally occurs in foods like tomatoes, parmesan, and soy sauce.
