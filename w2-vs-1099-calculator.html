Why a $100k Salary Does Not Equal a $100k Freelance Contract

When transitioning from corporate employment to independent contracting, one of the most common pitfalls is engaging in "salary matching." If an employee earning an $85,000 W-2 salary accepts an $85,000 1099 contract, they are taking an immediate and severe pay cut.

As a W-2 employee, your employer covers substantial hidden costs on your behalf. When you switch to 1099 status, all of those financial burdens shift entirely onto your shoulders.

1. The 15.3% Self-Employment Tax Trap

W-2 employees split their FICA taxes with their employers—you pay 7.65% from your paycheck, and your employer matches it with another 7.65%. As an independent contractor, you file Schedule SE and must pay the entire 15.3% yourself on 92.35% of your net self-employment earnings.

2. The Cost of Unpaid Time Off (PTO)

Corporate employees receive paid vacation days, federal holidays, and sick leave. Freelancers do not get paid when they take a day off. If you take 3 weeks of vacation and public holidays off, your annual billable capacity drops significantly, meaning your working hours must generate enough revenue to cover those non-working periods.

3. Valuing Employer-Provided Benefits

Health insurance premiums, dental plans, life insurance, and 401(k) matching contributions represent thousands of dollars in hidden compensation. A true freelance contract target must build in enough margin to purchase private health insurance and fund your own retirement accounts without an employer match.

Frequently Asked Questions

As a general rule of thumb, a 1099 freelancer needs to charge 25% to 35% more than an equivalent W-2 salary to take home the same net standard of living, largely due to self-employment taxes, paying for their own health insurance, and absorbing unpaid vacation days.
