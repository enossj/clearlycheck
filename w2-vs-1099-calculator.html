Why a $100k Salary Does Not Equal a $100k Freelance Contract

When transitioning from corporate employment to independent contracting, one of the most common—and most expensive—pitfalls is engaging in "salary matching." A software engineer earning $100,000 as a W-2 employee who accepts a $100,000 annual 1099 contract is not breaking even; they are taking an immediate and significant pay cut that can amount to $25,000 or more per year in lost compensation.

The reason is straightforward: as a W-2 employee, your employer absorbs a substantial portion of your total compensation cost behind the scenes. These hidden expenses include half of your FICA payroll taxes, the employer share of health insurance premiums, paid time off, workers' compensation insurance, unemployment insurance contributions (FUTA and SUTA), and often a matching contribution to your retirement plan. When you become a 1099 independent contractor, every single one of these financial obligations shifts directly onto your balance sheet. Understanding each component is essential to pricing your freelance services accurately.

The 15.3% Self-Employment Tax Trap

The single largest financial shock for new freelancers is the self-employment (SE) tax, which funds Social Security and Medicare. As a W-2 employee, your paycheck stub shows a 7.65% FICA deduction—6.2% for Social Security (on earnings up to the wage base, which is $176,100 for 2026) and 1.45% for Medicare. What you may not realize is that your employer quietly pays an identical 7.65% match on your behalf, bringing the total FICA contribution to 15.3% of your wages. You never see your employer's half because it never appears on your pay stub.

When you file taxes as a 1099 contractor, there is no employer to cover the other half. You are responsible for the entire 15.3%. The IRS requires you to calculate this via Schedule SE (Self-Employment Tax), which works as follows:

  • Step 1: Determine your net self-employment earnings (gross 1099 income minus allowable business deductions).
  • Step 2: Multiply net earnings by 92.35% (0.9235). The IRS allows this adjustment to simulate the fact that employers do not pay FICA on the employer-share portion itself. This taxable base is your "net earnings from self-employment."
  • Step 3: Apply the combined 15.3% rate (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare) to that adjusted figure.
  • Step 4: You may deduct 50% of the SE tax paid as an above-the-line adjustment on your Form 1040, reducing your adjusted gross income—but you still owe the full SE tax amount.

For example, on $100,000 of net self-employment income, you would compute: $100,000 × 0.9235 = $92,350 taxable base. Then $92,350 × 0.153 = $14,130 in self-employment tax. As a W-2 employee at the same income, you would have paid only $7,065 in FICA (7.65%), while your employer paid the other $7,065 invisibly. The net additional cost of going 1099 is therefore approximately $7,065 per year on a $100k income—money that must come from somewhere in your freelance pricing.

High earners face an additional wrinkle: the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on earned income above $200,000 (single filers) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). This surtax applies to both W-2 and 1099 income, but W-2 employees have it withheld automatically, while 1099 contractors must estimate and prepay it through quarterly estimated tax payments (Form 1040-ES) to avoid underpayment penalties.

The Cost of Unpaid Time Off (PTO)

Corporate employees rarely think about the monetary value of their paid time off because the money simply keeps arriving every pay period, even when they are on vacation. Consider a typical corporate benefits package: 10–15 vacation days, 10–11 federal holidays, and 3–5 sick days. Added together, that represents roughly 23–31 paid non-working days per year—or between 4.5 and 6 full working weeks.

A standard work year has approximately 260 weekdays (52 weeks × 5 days). If an employee takes 25 paid days off, they actually work 235 days, or about 1,880 hours (at 8 hours per day), while being paid for all 2,080 hours. For a freelancer, every day off is a day with zero revenue. If a 1099 contractor takes those same 25 days off, their annual billable capacity drops from 2,080 potential hours to roughly 1,640–1,800 billable hours (accounting for administrative time, business development, and invoicing overhead that W-2 employees never face).

This reduced billable capacity has a compounding effect. Not only must the freelancer earn their target annual income in fewer hours, but every non-billable hour (prospecting, contract negotiation, bookkeeping, continuing education) further erodes the denominator. Industry benchmarks suggest that independent consultants spend 15–25% of their total working time on non-billable administrative tasks. Our calculator defaults to 1,650 billable hours per year, which assumes approximately 3 weeks of vacation and holidays plus modest administrative overhead.

Valuing Employer-Provided Benefits

Beyond salary and payroll taxes, corporate employers provide a benefits package whose true replacement cost is frequently underestimated by aspiring freelancers. Understanding the dollar value of each component is critical to setting an accurate 1099 rate:

  • Health Insurance: According to the Kaiser Family Foundation's 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey, the average annual premium for employer-sponsored family health coverage was $25,572, with employers paying an average of 73% ($18,668). Single coverage averaged $9,084, with employers covering approximately 83% ($7,540). When you go freelance, you bear the entire premium—often at higher individual-market rates without the group pricing advantage that large employers negotiate.
  • Dental and Vision: Employer-sponsored dental plans typically cost $500–$800/year per employee; vision coverage adds another $100–$200. These are modest but add up when combined with other benefits.
  • Life and Disability Insurance: Many employers provide basic group life insurance (typically 1–2× annual salary) and short-term/long-term disability coverage at no cost to the employee. Purchasing equivalent individual policies on the open market can cost $1,000–$3,000/year depending on age, health, and coverage amount.
  • 401(k) Matching: A common employer match is 50% of contributions up to 6% of salary, or a dollar-for-dollar match up to 3–4%. On an $85,000 salary, a 4% match equals $3,400 in free retirement money per year. As a freelancer, you can contribute to a SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k), but there is no employer match—the entire contribution comes from your own revenue.
  • Other Perks: Professional development budgets, gym memberships, commuter benefits, employee stock purchase plans, and equipment stipends can collectively add $2,000–$5,000 in annual value depending on the employer.

When you total these hidden costs—$7,000+ in employer FICA match, $6,000–$18,000+ in health insurance, $3,000+ in retirement matching, and the cash value of 4–6 weeks of paid time off—a typical W-2 employee at an $85,000 salary is receiving total compensation closer to $110,000–$125,000. This is the number that your 1099 contract must match or exceed, not the base salary alone.

How This Calculator Works

Our W-2 vs. 1099 True Wage Calculator uses a straightforward methodology to determine the minimum gross 1099 contract you need to replace your W-2 compensation package:

  • Total W-2 Compensation: Base salary + employer health insurance value + PTO cash value (calculated as your daily rate × PTO days).
  • Gross-Up for SE Tax: The total compensation is divided by 0.8587 (which is 1 − [0.9235 × 0.153]) to reverse-engineer the gross contract amount needed before self-employment taxes consume their share.
  • Add Business Expenses: Annual overhead costs (software, hardware, co-working space, liability insurance) are added on top, since these are pre-deduction costs that come out of your gross revenue.
  • Hourly Rate: The total gross contract is divided by your estimated annual billable hours to produce a target hourly rate.

The result is a minimum floor—the gross contract amount that leaves you no better and no worse off than your W-2 position. In practice, most financial advisors recommend adding a 10–15% margin above this floor to account for income volatility, gaps between contracts, and the lack of unemployment insurance eligibility that W-2 employees enjoy.
