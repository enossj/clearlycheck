How many calories do I need each day?

There is no single calorie number that works for everyone. Your daily energy needs depend on factors such as age, body size, sex, metabolism, and physical activity. That is why a calorie target that works well for one person may be too high or too low for another.

Enter your own details above to get an estimate built around you. The calculator first estimates your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR), which is the energy your body would use at rest, then applies an activity factor to estimate your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE), also called your maintenance calories.

Think of your result as a starting point. Your real maintenance intake can be different from the calculator's estimate. Your weight trend over several weeks can help you judge whether the estimate is a good fit for your routine.

What are maintenance calories?

Your maintenance calories are an estimate of how much energy you need to maintain your current body weight when your usual activity stays about the same. In this calculator, maintenance is represented by your TDEE.

If you regularly eat less energy than you use, your body has to draw on stored energy. If you regularly eat more, the extra energy can contribute to weight gain. In real life, the relationship is not perfectly linear because appetite, activity, body weight, and energy expenditure can change over time. NIDDK's Body Weight Planner also accounts for changes in energy expenditure as body weight changes.

How many calories should I eat to lose weight?

For weight loss, the usual starting point is a calorie intake below your estimated maintenance level. The size of the deficit should be realistic enough that you can maintain your eating pattern and still meet your nutritional needs.

This calculator offers moderate goal settings rather than treating a large deficit as automatically better. NIDDK recommends setting achievable weight-loss goals and notes that individual calorie needs can differ.

Also remember that the simple idea that a fixed calorie deficit guarantees a fixed amount of weight loss every week is an oversimplification. As weight changes, energy expenditure can change too, so actual results may slow or differ from the initial estimate.

What is a calorie deficit?

A calorie deficit means your average calorie intake is lower than your energy expenditure. For example, if your estimated maintenance is 2,400 calories and you choose a 400-calorie deficit, your starting target would be about 2,000 calories per day.

The word "deficit" describes the relationship between intake and expenditure. It does not mean that the lowest possible calorie intake is the best choice. A sustainable eating pattern, adequate nutrition, and appropriate activity matter too.

What is BMR?

Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) is an estimate of the energy your body uses at rest to support basic functions. Your BMR is not the same as your recommended daily food intake because you also use energy for movement, exercise, digestion, and other daily activities.

This calculator estimates BMR with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation. It is a predictive equation, not a direct measurement of your metabolism. Laboratory methods such as indirect calorimetry can measure resting energy expenditure more directly, but they are not practical for a simple online calculator.

What is TDEE?

Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) is an estimate of the total energy you use across a typical day. It includes your resting energy needs plus movement, exercise, and the energy involved in processing food.

Your activity selection is therefore important. Someone with a desk job and little exercise can have very different daily energy needs from someone with the same height and weight who trains frequently or works a physically active job.

Why your calorie result may not match your real weight change

Calorie calculators use equations derived from groups of people. They cannot know every detail of your metabolism, daily movement, food intake, sleep, training, or changes in body composition.

  • Your activity level may be higher or lower than the category you selected.
  • Food portions and drinks can be harder to estimate than they appear.
  • Daily movement outside exercise can change from one period to another.
  • Water, glycogen, digestion, and other short-term changes can move the scale even when body fat has not changed.
  • As body weight changes, your energy needs can change as well.

That is why your calorie target should be treated as a starting estimate rather than a promise of a particular weight change.

How to use your result in real life

  1. Start with your estimated maintenance. This gives you a reference point for your current activity level.
  2. Choose a realistic goal. If you want to lose weight, start with a moderate reduction rather than assuming a larger deficit is always better.
  3. Give the plan time. Look at trends rather than judging your progress from a single day's scale reading.
  4. Recalculate when your circumstances change. A meaningful change in body weight or activity can change your estimated calorie needs.

Calories, protein, carbohydrates, and fat

Calories describe energy, while macronutrients describe where that energy comes from. Protein, carbohydrates, and fat each have different roles in the diet. The macro numbers shown in the calculator are a simple planning estimate, not a personalized medical nutrition prescription.

Use the macro breakdown as a way to understand your calorie target, not as a requirement that every day must match the exact gram numbers shown.

Calories burned during exercise

The activity table above gives estimates based on standard MET values and your entered body weight. Exercise calorie estimates are useful for understanding relative energy cost, but they are not exact measurements. Wearables and online exercise calculators can also differ because they use different assumptions.

Sources and methodology

This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation to estimate BMR and standard activity multipliers to estimate TDEE. The goal adjustment is then applied to the estimated maintenance calories. The result is intentionally presented as an estimate because individual energy requirements vary.

For personalized weight-management planning, the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases provides the NIH Body Weight Planner, which is designed to model calorie and physical-activity changes over time.

Important: This calculator is intended for general education. It is not designed for children, pregnancy, breastfeeding, eating-disorder treatment, or medical nutrition therapy.
