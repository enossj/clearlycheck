How this calorie calculator works

This calculator first estimates your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) — the calories your body burns at complete rest just to keep vital functions running — using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation:

SexFormula
MenBMR = 10 × weight(kg) + 6.25 × height(cm) − 5 × age + 5
WomenBMR = 10 × weight(kg) + 6.25 × height(cm) − 5 × age − 161

Your BMR is then multiplied by an activity factor to estimate your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) — the calories you burn in a typical day including movement and exercise. This is your maintenance calorie level. From there, a calorie surplus or deficit is applied based on your goal.

Why Mifflin-St Jeor instead of Harris-Benedict?

The Harris-Benedict equation, published in 1919 and revised in 1984, was the standard for decades. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation, published in 1990, was developed using more modern body composition data and has been shown in multiple studies to be more accurate for the general population, particularly for people with higher body fat percentages. It's the equation recommended by the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics for healthy adults.

Understanding calorie deficits and surpluses

One pound (0.45kg) of body fat is roughly equivalent to 3,500 calories. A daily deficit of 500 calories therefore works out to about one pound of weight loss per week — a pace considered sustainable and safe by most health guidelines. Faster deficits can produce quicker short-term results but are harder to maintain, more likely to cause muscle loss alongside fat loss, and more likely to trigger the kind of rebound weight regain that comes from an unsustainable approach.

For weight gain, the same logic applies in reverse: a moderate surplus supports gradual, mostly lean weight gain, while a large surplus tends to add more fat relative to muscle.

A note on minimum calorie floors

This calculator will not recommend a goal below approximately 1,200 calories/day for women or 1,500 calories/day for men, regardless of the deficit you select. Intakes below this range are difficult to meet nutritional needs with, and sustained very-low-calorie diets should only be undertaken under medical supervision. If your calculated goal would fall below this floor, consider a smaller deficit or speak with a healthcare provider about a safe, supervised plan.

Understanding your macro breakdown

The macro breakdown shown uses a balanced split of roughly 30% protein, 40% carbohydrates, and 30% fat — a reasonable general-purpose starting point for most goals. Protein and carbohydrates provide 4 calories per gram, while fat provides 9 calories per gram. Athletes, people doing significant strength training, or those with specific medical needs may benefit from a different split — a registered dietitian can help tailor this to you specifically.
