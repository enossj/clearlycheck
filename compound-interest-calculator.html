What is compound interest?

Compound interest is interest calculated on both the initial principal and the accumulated interest from previous periods. This is the key difference from simple interest, which only calculates on the original principal. Albert Einstein reportedly called compound interest the "eighth wonder of the world" — and the numbers back it up.

The compound interest formula

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)

A = Final amount
P = Principal (initial investment)
r = Annual interest rate (as a decimal, e.g. 0.07 for 7%)
n = Compounding periods per year (12 for monthly)
t = Time in years

For example: $10,000 at 7% compounded monthly for 10 years: A = 10,000 × (1 + 0.07/12)^(12×10) = $20,097

Why compounding frequency matters

The more frequently interest compounds, the more you earn — but the difference shrinks as frequency increases. Going from yearly to monthly compounding makes a meaningful difference. Going from monthly to daily makes almost no difference in practice.

  • Yearly compounding: $10,000 at 7% for 10 years → $19,672
  • Monthly compounding: $10,000 at 7% for 10 years → $20,097
  • Daily compounding: $10,000 at 7% for 10 years → $20,137

The Rule of 72

A quick mental shortcut: divide 72 by your annual interest rate to estimate how many years it takes to double your money. At 7% interest, your money doubles roughly every 72 ÷ 7 = 10.3 years. At 10%, every 7.2 years. This works because of how compound interest accelerates growth.

Why start early?

Time is the most powerful variable in compound interest. Someone who invests $10,000 at age 25 at 7% will have $149,745 at age 65. Someone who waits until 35 to invest the same amount will only have $76,123 — less than half — despite only 10 fewer years. Starting early matters more than the amount.
