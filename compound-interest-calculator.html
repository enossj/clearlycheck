What is compound interest?

Compound interest is interest earned on your original money and on interest that has already been added to the balance. That second part is what gives compounding its momentum. Investor.gov describes compound interest as interest paid on principal and accumulated interest.

For a saver, the important idea is simple: money that stays invested has more time to generate its own future earnings. Your actual result still depends on the rate, fees, taxes, contributions, and how long you keep the money invested.

Think in layers: starting money creates the first layer of growth, recurring contributions add more principal, and the interest on both can continue compounding.

How to calculate compound interest

For a single lump sum with a fixed nominal annual rate, the common compound-growth formula is:

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t)

P = starting principal
r = annual rate as a decimal
n = compounding periods per year
t = time in years
A = ending value

Once regular deposits are added, the calculation also has to account for when each deposit enters the account. That is why a compound-interest calculator with contributions is more useful than applying the lump-sum formula to the entire balance.

Compound interest vs. simple interest

Simple interest grows from the original principal only. Compound growth lets prior interest remain in the balance and participate in future growth.

Feature Simple interest Compound interest
Interest calculated on Original principal Principal plus accumulated interest
Growth pattern More linear Compounding effect over time
Regular contributions Handled separately Each contribution can earn future interest

How monthly contributions affect compound growth

A recurring contribution changes two things at once: you add more principal, and each deposit gets its own opportunity to earn future growth. Investor.gov and NerdWallet both include recurring contributions in their compound-growth examples because regular additions can materially change the outcome.

Try changing the contribution from $100 to $500 while keeping every other input the same. The calculator makes the difference visible without assuming that a larger contribution is automatically affordable for you.

Does contribution timing matter?

Yes. A contribution made at the beginning of a period has more time to earn under the model than the same contribution made at the end of the period. Calculator.net exposes beginning-versus-end contribution timing because it can change the final balance.

The difference is usually much smaller than the effect of changing the contribution amount or investment horizon, but it is worth including when you want a more careful estimate.

Why compounding frequency matters

Daily, monthly, quarterly, and annual compounding can produce different results when the stated nominal rate is the same. More frequent compounding generally increases the effective annual yield, but the incremental benefit narrows as compounding becomes frequent.

For recurring contributions, this calculator derives the equivalent annual effect of the selected compounding schedule and then models contributions at your chosen frequency. That keeps the comparison consistent while making the assumptions visible.

How much will $10,000 grow?

There is no single answer because the rate and time period matter. At 7% compounded monthly with no additional contributions, $10,000 grows to about $20,097 after 10 years. Change the rate, add monthly contributions, or shorten the horizon and the result changes immediately.

Useful habit: run the same starting amount at several reasonable rates instead of treating one assumed return as a forecast. The calculator's scenario comparison is designed for this.

What is the Rule of 72?

The Rule of 72 is a quick mental shortcut. Divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate how many years it may take an investment to double. At 7%, the estimate is about 10.3 years.

It is only a shortcut. The exact compound-growth result depends on the rate, compounding, contributions, fees, taxes, and the period being modeled. Investor.gov also presents the Rule of 72 as an estimation tool rather than an exact calculation.

Interest rate vs. APY

These terms are related but not identical. A nominal interest rate states a yearly rate before the effect of intra-year compounding is reflected. APY incorporates compounding into the annualized yield. When you enter a rate into a calculator, check whether the rate you have is a stated interest rate or an APY so you do not accidentally compound twice.

Inflation: what will the future balance buy?

A future balance is a dollar amount in the future, not a guarantee of today's purchasing power. The optional inflation input estimates a future balance's purchasing power in today's dollars by discounting the projected value at the assumed inflation rate.

This is a planning estimate. It does not predict future inflation, and the inflation rate for your household spending can be different from a broad inflation measure.

How taxes can change compound growth

Interest, dividends, and investment gains can be taxed differently depending on the account and tax rules that apply. The optional tax input on this page is deliberately simplified: it applies the entered percentage to modeled interest as a planning illustration. It is not a tax-return calculation.

Use the Savings Goal mode to work backward

Most people start with a balance and ask, “How much could this become?” A second, often more useful question is, “How much do I need to contribute to reach my goal?” Investor.gov provides a separate Savings Goal Calculator for this reverse calculation.

Choose Savings Goal, enter your target amount and time horizon, and the calculator estimates the regular contribution required under the assumptions you provide.

Use Time to Goal when the contribution is fixed

If you know what you can regularly save but do not know how long the journey may take, use Time to Goal. Enter the target, starting amount, contribution and assumed rate. The calculator searches for the estimated time needed to reach that goal.

How to use the result in real life

  1. Start with your actual starting balance. A realistic number is more useful than a round number you may never invest.
  2. Use a rate assumption you can defend. Compare several scenarios rather than relying on one optimistic number.
  3. Test your contribution. Small recurring amounts can matter over long periods, but only if the contribution is sustainable.
  4. Check inflation. A large future balance can still have less purchasing power than the number suggests.
  5. Separate planning from prediction. The output is a model based on your assumptions, not a forecast of what a particular investment will actually earn.

Sources and methodology

This calculator uses standard compound-growth relationships for a fixed nominal rate and recurring contributions. For regular contributions, the model converts the selected compounding schedule into an equivalent annual growth rate and applies that rate to the selected contribution frequency and timing. Inflation and tax adjustments are optional planning layers.
