The Complexities of Global Time Zones

The world is divided into 24 primary time zones, each roughly 15 degrees of longitude wide, radiating outward from the prime meridian at Greenwich, England. In theory, this creates a clean grid where each zone is exactly one hour offset from its neighbors. In practice, time zones are shaped by national borders, political decisions, and historical conventions that make the global time map far more complex than a simple 24-slice pie.

All civil time zones are defined as a positive or negative offset from Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), the world's primary time standard. UTC is maintained by a global network of over 400 atomic clocks and does not observe Daylight Saving Time. It replaced Greenwich Mean Time (GMT) as the international reference point in 1960, though the terms are still often used interchangeably in casual conversation. Technically, GMT is a time zone (UTC+0), while UTC is the time standard itself.

Offsets range from UTC−12 (Baker Island, uninhabited) to UTC+14 (Line Islands, Kiribati), meaning there are actually 26+ hours of difference around the globe at any given moment. Some offsets are not whole hours: India uses UTC+5:30, Nepal uses UTC+5:45, and the Chatham Islands (New Zealand) use UTC+12:45. These fractional offsets create additional complexity when computing overlap windows.

To further complicate matters, some countries span multiple time zones while choosing to use just one. China, despite stretching across five geographical time zones (from UTC+5 to UTC+9), uses a single national standard: UTC+8 (Beijing Time). This means that sunrise in Kashgar (western China) can occur as late as 10:00 AM local time in winter. India similarly uses a single timezone (UTC+5:30) across its entire breadth, even though the country spans nearly 30 degrees of longitude.

Navigating Daylight Saving Time (DST) Traps

Daylight Saving Time is perhaps the single greatest source of scheduling errors in international business. DST shifts clocks forward by one hour in spring ("spring forward") and back by one hour in autumn ("fall back"), but the critical complication is that different countries change on different dates—and many skip DST entirely.

Here is how DST transition dates vary across major regions:

  • United States and Canada: Spring forward on the second Sunday of March; fall back on the first Sunday of November.
  • European Union: Spring forward on the last Sunday of March; fall back on the last Sunday of October.
  • Australia (SE states): Spring forward on the first Sunday of October; fall back on the first Sunday of April. (Note: this is the opposite season from the Northern Hemisphere.)
  • No DST observed: Most of Asia (India, China, Japan, South Korea, Singapore), most of Africa, most of South America, Arizona (US), Hawaii (US), and Iceland.

The mismatch in transition dates creates dangerous "gap weeks." For example, in 2026 the US springs forward on March 8, but Europe does not spring forward until March 29. During those three weeks, the US–UK time difference is 4 hours instead of the usual 5. If your team has a standing 9:00 AM ET / 2:00 PM GMT meeting, it will temporarily become 9:00 AM ET / 1:00 PM GMT without anyone realizing it unless the calendar software handles IANA timezone identifiers (more on that below). This is the most common source of "I showed up an hour early/late" confusion in globally distributed teams.

Arizona (except the Navajo Nation) does not observe DST. This means Arizona is on Mountain Standard Time (UTC−7) year-round, which matches Pacific Daylight Time (UTC−7) in summer but Mountain Standard Time in winter, while neighboring states shift around it. India, Japan, and China never change their clocks, making them reliable anchors for scheduling—but also meaning the US–India and US–Japan time gaps vary by one hour depending on whether the US is on standard or daylight time.

Best Practices for Scheduling International Remote Meetings

Finding the elusive "golden hours" of overlap for distributed teams requires a combination of tooling, empathy, and process discipline. Here are actionable strategies used by successful remote-first companies:

1. Map the Overlap Windows First

Before scheduling anything, use a timezone planner (like this one) to identify the hours when all participants are within their working day. Common overlap windows for frequently paired regions:

  • US East Coast + UK/Europe: 9:00 AM – 1:00 PM ET (2:00 – 6:00 PM GMT / 3:00 – 7:00 PM CET) — 4 hours of clean overlap.
  • US East Coast + India (IST): 8:00 – 9:30 AM ET (6:30 – 8:00 PM IST) — a tight 1.5-hour window, the only practical slot given the 10.5-hour offset.
  • US West Coast + India: Essentially no overlap during standard business hours (13.5-hour gap). One side must work outside normal hours.
  • UK + India: 9:00 AM – 1:30 PM GMT (2:30 – 7:00 PM IST) — a comfortable 4.5-hour window.
  • US + Australia (Sydney): Very difficult. Sydney is 14–16 hours ahead of the US West Coast depending on DST. The only overlap is late US evening / early Australian morning.

2. Rotate the Inconvenient Slot

When overlap requires one person to join at 7:00 AM or 8:00 PM, establish a rotation so no single team member consistently bears the burden of early morning or late evening calls. Document the rotation in a shared calendar to ensure fairness.

3. Default to Async-First Communication

Many meetings can be replaced by a 5-minute recorded video update (Loom, Vidyard), a written brief in Notion or Confluence, or a structured Slack thread. Reserve synchronous meetings for decisions that require real-time discussion, brainstorming, or sensitive conversations that benefit from live tone and context.

4. Always Specify Timezone in Invitations

Never write "3:00 PM" in a meeting invite without specifying the timezone. Best practice: include both the organizer's local time and UTC, e.g., "3:00 PM EST (20:00 UTC)." Better yet, use calendar tools that automatically convert to each recipient's local time.

5. Use IANA Timezone Identifiers

Timezone abbreviations like "EST" and "IST" are ambiguous—IST can mean India Standard Time, Irish Standard Time, or Israel Standard Time. The IANA timezone database (also called the tz database or Olson database) uses unambiguous identifiers like America/New_York, Europe/London, and Asia/Kolkata. These identifiers encode both the base UTC offset and all historical and future DST transition rules. Our planner uses IANA identifiers via the browser's built-in Intl API, ensuring that time conversions are accurate even during DST transition periods.

6. Account for Half-Hour and Quarter-Hour Offsets

India (UTC+5:30), Nepal (UTC+5:45), Iran (UTC+3:30), Afghanistan (UTC+4:30), Myanmar (UTC+6:30), and the Chatham Islands (UTC+12:45) all use non-whole-hour offsets. When scheduling with participants in these regions, a "9:00 AM" meeting in New York starts at 7:30 PM in Mumbai, not 8:00 PM. Failing to account for the 30-minute offset is a surprisingly common error.

How This Planner Works

Our Timezone Meeting Planner uses the browser's native Intl.DateTimeFormat API with IANA timezone identifiers to perform all time conversions. Here is the process:

  • City selection: You pick up to 5 cities from our database of 35+ major global hubs. Each city maps to an IANA timezone (e.g., "New York" → America/New_York).
  • Working hours definition: You set the start and end of the business day (default 9 AM – 6 PM).
  • Overlap computation: For each UTC hour (0–23), the planner converts to each city's local time and checks whether all cities are within working hours. Consecutive overlapping hours are grouped into "meeting windows."
  • 24-hour timeline: A visual grid shows working hours (light green), overlap windows (dark green), and off-hours (gray) for every selected city, providing an at-a-glance view of the entire day.
  • Copy summary: One click copies a formatted meeting summary with all cities' current times, ready to paste into Slack, email, or a calendar invitation.
