What is sales tax?

Sales tax is a percentage that a government adds to the price of goods and many services, collected by the seller at the point of sale and passed on to the state or local authority. In the United States there is no national sales tax — each state sets its own rate, and counties and cities often stack additional local taxes on top. That's why the same item can cost slightly different amounts at checkout depending on where you buy it.

How to calculate sales tax on a purchase

To add sales tax to a price, convert the rate to a decimal (divide by 100), multiply it by the price to get the tax, then add that to the price:

  • Tax amount = price × (rate ÷ 100)
  • Total = price + tax amount

Example: a $100 item at a 7.25% rate. Tax = $100 × 0.0725 = $7.25, so the total is $107.25.

How to back out sales tax from a total

Sometimes you have the final tax-included total and want to know the original price — for expense reports, receipts that don't itemize tax, or bookkeeping. Divide the total by 1 plus the rate as a decimal:

  • Pre-tax price = total ÷ (1 + rate ÷ 100)
  • Tax portion = total − pre-tax price

Example: a $107.25 total at 7.25%. Pre-tax price = $107.25 ÷ 1.0725 = $100.00, so the tax portion was $7.25. A common mistake is to simply subtract 7.25% of the total — that gives the wrong answer, because the tax was calculated on the smaller pre-tax figure, not the total.

Sales tax vs. VAT

Outside the US, most countries use a value-added tax (VAT) instead of sales tax. The key difference is where the tax lands: US sales tax is charged once, at the final sale to the consumer, and is added at the register. VAT is collected in stages along the supply chain and is almost always already baked into the shelf price. For a shopper the end cost can be similar, but a US price tag usually understates what you'll actually pay, while a European one usually doesn't.

Why rates vary so much by location

Your checkout rate is typically a combination of three layers: the state rate, an optional county rate, and an optional city or district rate. A state with a modest base rate can end up with one of the highest combined rates once local taxes are added, and vice versa. Some categories — groceries, prescription drugs, clothing — are taxed at reduced rates or exempted entirely in certain states, so the calculator gives you the general figure; always check local rules for special categories.
