How this income tax calculator works

This calculator estimates your federal income tax using the same building blocks as an actual return: your wages and other income form your gross income, adjustments reduce that to your adjusted gross income (AGI), and then either the standard deduction or your itemized deductions — whichever is larger — reduces AGI down to your taxable income. Federal tax is then calculated progressively across the seven IRS tax brackets, and finally reduced by the Child Tax Credit and Credit for Other Dependents, if applicable. Use the tax year selector on the left to switch between the 2025 return (filed in 2026) and the 2026 return (filed in 2027).

Standard deduction

Most taxpayers take the standard deduction rather than itemizing. For 2026, the standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers, $32,200 for married couples filing jointly, and $24,150 for heads of household. For 2025, it's $15,750 single, $31,500 joint, and $23,625 head of household. Taxpayers 65 or older can claim an additional standard deduction on top of this — $2,050 per qualifying person (single/HoH) or $1,650 per qualifying spouse (joint) for 2026; $2,000 and $1,600 respectively for 2025.

Taxable income vs. other taxable income

Taxable income is the final number tax brackets are applied to — your income after adjustments and deductions. But not all income is taxed the same way to get there. Wages (W-2 Box 1) are straightforward. Other taxable income is a broader bucket that includes interest, ordinary dividends, unemployment compensation, retirement plan distributions, and self-employment or business income. This calculator groups all of it together and applies ordinary rates, which is accurate for most of these income types but is a simplification for qualified dividends and long-term capital gains (see below).

Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI)

Several tax rules — including the Child Tax Credit phaseout modeled in this calculator — don't use your plain AGI, they use Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI). MAGI starts with AGI and adds back certain items that were excluded or deducted, most commonly the foreign earned income exclusion, tax-exempt interest, and some IRA deductions. For most taxpayers without foreign income or unusual deductions, MAGI and AGI are the same number, which is why this calculator uses AGI as a close approximation. If you have foreign income or excluded interest, your actual MAGI-based phaseouts may differ slightly from this estimate.

Above-the-line vs. below-the-line deductions

Tax deductions fall into two categories, named for where they appear on Form 1040. Above-the-line deductions — technically "adjustments to income" — reduce your gross income down to your AGI, and you can claim them whether or not you itemize. Common examples include traditional IRA contributions, student loan interest (up to $2,500), HSA contributions, and educator expenses. This calculator's "Adjustments to income" field covers this category.

Below-the-line deductions are the standard deduction or itemized deductions, applied after AGI to arrive at taxable income. Common itemized deductions include mortgage interest, state and local taxes (SALT, capped at $10,000 for most filers), charitable donations, and medical expenses exceeding 7.5% of AGI. You claim either the standard deduction or the total of your itemized deductions — whichever is larger — never both.

Child Tax Credit and Credit for Other Dependents

The Child Tax Credit is worth up to $2,200 per qualifying child under age 17 in both 2025 and 2026 (unchanged, per OBBBA). Dependents who don't qualify for the Child Tax Credit — such as older children or other relatives you support — may qualify for the $500 Credit for Other Dependents instead. Both credits begin phasing out once adjusted gross income exceeds $200,000 ($400,000 for joint filers), reducing the combined credit by $50 for every $1,000 over that threshold. Up to $1,700 of the Child Tax Credit is refundable, meaning you can receive it even if it exceeds your tax liability — this calculator applies the full credit as non-refundable for simplicity, so very low-income filers with children may see a larger actual refund than shown here.

Common tax credits

Credits reduce your tax bill dollar-for-dollar, which makes them more valuable than an equivalent deduction. Beyond the Child Tax Credit and Credit for Other Dependents modeled in this calculator, other common federal credits include:

  • Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) — a refundable credit for low-to-moderate income workers, worth up to $8,231 for three or more qualifying children in 2026.
  • Child and Dependent Care Credit — for costs of care that allow you to work, such as daycare or a nanny.
  • American Opportunity & Lifetime Learning Credits — for qualified college and continuing education expenses.
  • Saver's Credit — up to 50% of retirement contributions for low-to-moderate income savers.
  • Residential Clean Energy Credit — for solar, geothermal, and other qualifying home energy improvements.

These aren't included in this calculator's math since each has its own income limits and qualifying rules — but if any apply to you, your actual tax owed will likely be lower than this estimate.

Business income and self-employment

If part of your "other income" comes from self-employment or a small business, two things apply that this calculator doesn't model. First, self-employment tax — 15.3% covering Social Security and Medicare — is owed in addition to income tax on net self-employment earnings. Second, many self-employed and pass-through business owners can claim the Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction of up to 20% of qualified business income, which lowers taxable income before brackets are applied. Both can meaningfully change your actual liability, so self-employed filers should treat this calculator's estimate as a rough starting point only.

The Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT)

The AMT is a parallel tax system originally designed to ensure high earners couldn't reduce their tax bill to nearly zero through deductions. Under the AMT, certain deductions aren't allowed, and a flat 26% or 28% rate applies above an exemption amount. For 2026, the AMT exemption is $90,100 for single filers and $140,200 for joint filers, phasing out above $500,000 and $1,000,000 respectively. Because OBBBA raised standard deductions and simplified several rules, the AMT now affects a relatively small share of taxpayers — mostly those with large state tax deductions, incentive stock options, or high itemized deductions in high-income years. This calculator doesn't compute AMT; if you have any of these situations, a tax professional can tell you whether it applies to you.

What this calculator doesn't cover

  • State income tax — rules and rates vary by state, and several states have none at all.
  • Self-employment tax — the 15.3% Social Security and Medicare tax owed by self-employed workers.
  • The Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT) — a parallel tax system that affects a small number of higher-income taxpayers.
  • Capital gains and qualified dividend rates — these are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% rather than ordinary income rates; this calculator applies ordinary rates to all "other income" as a simplification.
  • The Earned Income Tax Credit and other income-limited credits — the EITC, education credits, and Saver's Credit all have their own phaseout rules not modeled here.

For a full, accurate calculation of your specific situation, use IRS Free File, reputable tax software, or a licensed tax preparer.
