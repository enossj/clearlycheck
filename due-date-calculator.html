How due dates are calculated

The most widely used method is Naegele's Rule, developed in the 19th century: take the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP) and add 280 days (40 weeks). This assumes a 28-day cycle with ovulation around day 14 — if your cycle is longer or shorter, the estimate shifts accordingly, which is why this calculator lets you adjust for your actual cycle length.

Pregnancy is conventionally measured in weeks from the LMP, not from the actual date of conception — which is a common source of confusion. This means "40 weeks pregnant" is really about 38 weeks since conception for someone with an average cycle.

Why your due date might change

It's common for an estimated due date to shift slightly as pregnancy progresses, especially after an early ultrasound. Ultrasound measurements taken in the first trimester (before about 14 weeks) are generally considered the most accurate way to date a pregnancy, more so than LMP-based estimates alone — particularly for anyone with irregular cycles or an uncertain last period date. If your provider gives you a different date than this calculator, their estimate — especially if based on an early scan — is usually the one to go with.

Sources & method

Due dates are estimated using Naegele's rule (last menstrual period + 280 days), the standard method used by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (acog.org). Ultrasound and IVF dating options adjust from the relevant reference date. This is an estimate — only about 5% of babies arrive on the exact due date.
