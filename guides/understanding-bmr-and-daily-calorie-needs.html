Understanding BMR and Daily Calorie Needs

How many calories does your body actually need? We walk through the Mifflin-St Jeor equation step-by-step to reveal the math behind your metabolism, weight loss, and muscle gain.

"How many calories should I eat?" is one of the most Googled health questions on the internet—and it is consistently answered with terrible advice. The vague "2,000 calories a day" figure plastered on the back of every nutrition label is an arbitrary average created in the 1990s. Telling everyone to eat 2,000 calories is like telling everyone to wear a size 9 shoe.

Your actual, biological calorie needs are highly personal. They depend on four measurable inputs: your exact weight, your height, your age, and how much you move. If you want to lose fat, build muscle, or just maintain your current physique without guessing, you have to understand the underlying math of your metabolism.

This guide explains exactly how to calculate your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) and your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE)—the only two numbers that actually matter when programming your nutrition.

What Is BMR? (Your "Coma Calories")

Your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) is the number of calories your body burns at complete, absolute rest just to keep you alive. Think of it as your "coma calories." It is the energy required for breathing, pumping blood, maintaining body temperature, repairing cells, and keeping your brain functioning.

Many people are shocked to learn that BMR typically accounts for 60% to 75% of the total calories you burn in a day. Your organs are incredibly energy-hungry. Even if you lie in bed staring at the ceiling for 24 hours, your liver, brain, and skeletal muscles are constantly burning calories.

This is why extreme "crash diets" that drop your intake to 800 calories a day are so dangerous. You are effectively starving your own organs of the energy they need to perform basic maintenance.

The Mifflin-St Jeor Equation

Several mathematical formulas exist to estimate BMR. For decades, the Harris-Benedict equation was the gold standard. However, in 1990, a more accurate formula was developed: the Mifflin-St Jeor equation. Today, clinical dietitians, sports nutritionists, and our own calculators use this formula because it has proven to be the most accurate predictor of metabolic rate for modern adults.

For Women: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age in years) − 161 For Men: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age in years) + 5

The only difference between the male and female formulas is the final constant (+5 vs −161). This accounts for the biological average that men typically carry slightly more lean muscle mass than women at the same height and weight.

Step 1: Convert your stats to metric

Let's use a concrete example: a 35-year-old woman who is 5 feet 6 inches tall and weighs 150 pounds.

Weight: 150 lbs ÷ 2.205 = 68.0 kg
Height: 66 inches × 2.54 = 167.6 cm
Age: 35 years

Step 2: Apply the female formula

BMR = (10 × 68.0) + (6.25 × 167.6) − (5 × 35) − 161

BMR = 680 + 1,047.5 − 175 − 161

BMR = 1,391.5 calories/day

This woman burns 1,391 calories a day just existing. It is her metabolic floor.

From BMR to TDEE (Adding Activity)

BMR alone doesn't tell you how much to eat, because you (hopefully) do not spend your entire life in bed. You burn additional calories through exercise, walking to the car, digesting your food, and fidgeting at your desk.

When you combine your BMR with your daily movement, you get your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE). To find this, you multiply your BMR by an "activity multiplier."

Activity Level Description Multiplier TDEE (Our Example)
Sedentary Desk job, little to no formal exercise. BMR × 1.2 1,670 cal/day
Lightly Active Light exercise or sports 1–3 days a week. BMR × 1.375 1,913 cal/day
Moderately Active Moderate exercise or sports 3–5 days a week. BMR × 1.55 2,157 cal/day
Very Active Hard exercise or sports 6–7 days a week. BMR × 1.725 2,400 cal/day
Extremely Active Very hard physical labor + intense training daily. BMR × 1.9 2,644 cal/day

If our 35-year-old woman goes to the gym for 45 minutes four times a week, she is "Moderately Active." Her maintenance calories are 2,157 a day. If she eats exactly 2,157 calories, her weight will not change.

The Magic of NEAT

When calculating TDEE, many people drastically overestimate how many calories they burn in the gym, while completely ignoring NEAT (Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis).

NEAT represents the calories you burn doing everything that isn't sleeping, eating, or formal exercise. It includes walking your dog, pacing while on a phone call, tapping your foot, and carrying groceries. For many people, NEAT burns significantly more calories throughout the week than three 45-minute gym sessions combined.

The Fitness Tracker Trap: Be very careful trusting the "calories burned" metric on your Apple Watch or Fitbit. Studies consistently show that wrist-based fitness trackers overestimate calorie burn by 20% to 90%. If your watch says you burned 600 calories on the treadmill, and you "eat those calories back" via a post-workout meal, you will likely gain weight. Stick to your calculated TDEE instead.

BMI vs. BMR: Know the Difference

People often confuse BMR with BMI (Body Mass Index). They sound similar, but they measure completely different things.

While BMR calculates your energy engine (calories), BMI is simply a ratio of your weight to your height. It is a rapid screening tool used by doctors to classify you into categories: Underweight, Normal, Overweight, or Obese.

The flaw with BMI is that it cannot differentiate between bone, fat, and muscle. A 220-pound professional bodybuilder with 8% body fat will be classified as "Obese" on a BMI chart, which is obviously inaccurate. Use BMI as a rough, general health indicator, but use BMR/TDEE to actually plan your diet.

Setting Your Calorie Targets

Once you know your TDEE (maintenance level), achieving your physical goals becomes simple mathematics.

For Weight Loss (The Deficit)

To lose weight, you must consume fewer calories than your TDEE. A universally accepted rule of thumb is that one pound of fat contains roughly 3,500 calories. Therefore, a daily deficit of 500 calories will result in about 1 pound of fat loss per week (500 × 7 days = 3,500).

  • Aggressive Loss (1 lb/week): TDEE − 500 calories.
  • Steady Loss (0.5 lb/week): TDEE − 250 calories.

Warning: Women should generally not drop below 1,200 calories a day, and men should not drop below 1,500 calories a day without medical supervision.

For Muscle Gain (The Surplus)

To build lean muscle without packing on excess body fat, you need a slight surplus combined with heavy resistance training.

  • Lean Bulk: TDEE + 250 to 300 calories.

The "Starvation Mode" Myth

You have probably heard that if you eat too few calories, your body will enter "starvation mode" and hold onto fat. This is a severe misunderstanding of a real biological process called metabolic adaptation.

If you diet too aggressively, your body does not magically create fat out of thin air. Instead, it becomes highly efficient at saving energy. Your BMR drops slightly because a smaller body requires less energy to maintain. More importantly, your body subconsciously turns down your NEAT. You will fidget less, you will feel lethargic, and you will sit down more often. You stop losing weight not because of "starvation mode," but because your TDEE has plummeted to match your low calorie intake.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is basal metabolic rate (BMR)?
BMR is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest to maintain basic life-sustaining functions—breathing, circulation, temperature regulation, and cellular repair. It accounts for 60% to 75% of your total daily calorie burn.
How do I calculate my total daily calorie needs?
First, calculate your BMR using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation. Then, multiply that BMR by an activity factor (ranging from 1.2 for sedentary desk jobs up to 1.9 for intense daily athletes) to find your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE). Eat at your TDEE to maintain weight, below it to lose, and above it to gain.
How many calories should I eat to lose weight safely?
Subtract 250 to 500 calories from your TDEE. A 500-calorie daily deficit produces roughly 1 pound of weight loss per week. A 250-calorie deficit produces about 0.5 pounds per week. Drastic deficits usually lead to muscle loss and severe metabolic adaptation.
How accurate is the Mifflin-St Jeor equation?
Extensive clinical studies show it predicts measured BMR within ±10% for most healthy adults, making it more accurate than older formulas like Harris-Benedict. However, it is an estimate. Very muscular individuals may have a higher true BMR, while those with very high body fat may have a slightly lower one.
Does my BMR go down as I lose weight?
Yes. As you lose body mass (both fat and muscle), you become a smaller organism. A smaller engine requires less fuel to idle. This is why you must recalculate your BMR/TDEE for every 10 to 15 pounds of weight you lose to ensure you remain in a calorie deficit.
Should I eat back the calories I burn during exercise?
Generally, no. If you used an activity multiplier (like "Moderately Active") to calculate your TDEE, your exercise calories are already factored into your daily target. Eating them back would result in double-counting, erasing your calorie deficit.
Can I naturally increase my BMR?
Yes. Muscle tissue is more metabolically active than fat tissue. By engaging in resistance training (weightlifting) and building lean muscle mass, you permanently increase the number of calories your body burns at rest.
What is reverse dieting?
If you have been eating in a severe calorie deficit for a long time, your metabolism adapts and slows down. Reverse dieting is the process of slowly adding 50 to 100 calories back into your daily intake each week to safely restore your metabolism to a normal TDEE level without rapidly gaining fat.
