Understanding BMR and Daily Calorie Needs

How many calories does your body actually need? We walk through the Mifflin-St Jeor equation step by step — the same formula used by dietitians and our calorie calculator.

Built & reviewed by Enos, ClearlyCheck · Last updated August 31, 2026 · 7 min read

"How many calories should I eat?" is one of the most Googled health questions — and one of the most poorly answered. The vague "2,000 calories a day" figure on food labels is an average for an average person, which means it's wrong for almost everyone reading it. Your actual calorie needs depend on four measurable inputs: your weight, height, age, and how active you are.

This guide explains exactly how to calculate your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) and your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) — the two numbers that actually matter for any weight or nutrition goal.

What Is BMR?

Your Basal Metabolic Rate is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest just to keep you alive — breathing, pumping blood, maintaining body temperature, repairing cells, and running your organs. It's what you'd burn if you lay in bed doing nothing for 24 hours.

BMR typically accounts for 60–70% of the total calories most people burn in a day. Even if you're sedentary, the majority of your calorie expenditure comes from these involuntary processes. This is why crash diets that drop calories dramatically often backfire — the body adapts by slowing BMR over time.

The Mifflin-St Jeor Equation

Several formulas exist to estimate BMR. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation, developed in 1990, is consistently rated as the most accurate for most adults in clinical research and is the formula used by our calorie calculator.

For women: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age in years) − 161 For men: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age in years) + 5

The only difference between the male and female formulas is the final constant (+5 vs −161), which accounts for average differences in body composition at the same weight and height.

Worked Example: Calculating BMR

Let's use a concrete example: a 35-year-old woman who is 5 feet 6 inches tall (167.6 cm) and weighs 150 pounds (68 kg).

Step 1 — Convert to metric

Weight: 150 lbs ÷ 2.205 = 68.0 kg

Height: 5'6" = 66 inches × 2.54 = 167.6 cm

Age: 35 years

Step 2 — Apply the Mifflin-St Jeor formula (female)

BMR = (10 × 68.0) + (6.25 × 167.6) − (5 × 35) − 161

BMR = 680 + 1,047.5 − 175 − 161

BMR = 1,391.5 calories/day

This is what her body burns at complete rest. It's the absolute floor — she burns at least this many calories every single day regardless of activity.

From BMR to TDEE: Adding Activity

BMR alone doesn't tell you how much to eat. You also burn calories through movement, exercise, digestion (the thermic effect of food), and non-exercise activity (fidgeting, standing, walking around). Together these are captured in your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE).

TDEE is calculated by multiplying BMR by an activity factor:

Activity LevelDescriptionMultiplierTDEE (our example)
SedentaryDesk job, little or no exercise× 1.21,670 cal/day
Lightly activeLight exercise 1–3 days/week× 1.3751,913 cal/day
Moderately activeModerate exercise 3–5 days/week× 1.552,157 cal/day
Very activeHard exercise 6–7 days/week× 1.7252,400 cal/day
Extremely activePhysical job + intense training× 1.92,644 cal/day

If our example woman exercises moderately three to four times per week, her TDEE is approximately 2,157 calories/day. Eating this amount maintains her current weight. Less than this causes weight loss; more causes weight gain.

The Key Number

TDEE is your maintenance calories — the level at which your weight stays stable. Eat below it to lose weight, above it to gain. A 500-calorie daily deficit creates roughly a 1-pound loss per week. A 250-calorie deficit creates a 0.5-pound loss per week.

How BMI Fits In

BMI (Body Mass Index) is a separate metric that classifies your weight relative to your height — it is not a calorie calculation. The formula is:

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height² (m²) Our example: 68.0 ÷ (1.676)² = 68.0 ÷ 2.809 = 24.2

A BMI of 24.2 falls in the Normal weight category (18.5–24.9) according to World Health Organization classifications. The full scale:

BMI RangeWHO Classification
Below 18.5Underweight
18.5 – 24.9Normal weight
25.0 – 29.9Overweight
30.0 and aboveObese

BMI has known limitations: it doesn't distinguish between muscle and fat, which means it can overclassify muscular athletes as overweight and underclassify people with low muscle mass. Use it as a rough screening tool, not a definitive health verdict. Our BMI calculator shows your classification alongside an explanation of what it does and doesn't mean.

Putting It Together: Calorie Targets for Common Goals

Weight loss

Subtract 250–500 calories from your TDEE. Using our example at moderate activity (TDEE 2,157): a 500-calorie deficit gives a target of 1,657 cal/day, aiming for ~1 lb loss per week. Most health professionals advise staying above 1,200 cal/day for women without medical supervision.

Weight gain / muscle building

Add 250–500 calories above TDEE. A surplus of 300 cal/day paired with resistance training supports lean muscle gain while minimising fat gain.

Weight maintenance

Eat at TDEE. This is harder than it sounds — most people don't realise how many calories are in the foods they eat regularly, which is why tracking for even a few weeks is eye-opening.

Why These Are Estimates, Not Exact Numbers

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation estimates BMR with roughly ±10% accuracy for most people, and the activity multipliers add more variation on top. Individual factors — genetics, hormones, gut microbiome, sleep quality, and medication — all affect actual calorie burn in ways no formula can fully capture.

Treat your calculated TDEE as a starting point. Track your actual intake against it for two to four weeks, observe whether your weight moves as expected, and adjust accordingly. Most people find their real maintenance calories within 100–200 calories of their TDEE estimate after a few weeks of tracking.
