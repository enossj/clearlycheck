Car Lease vs. Buy: Making the Right Choice

The lease payment is undeniably lower—but what does it actually cost you over a decade? We run the real numbers on a $35,000 car so you can navigate the dealership without getting played.

Walk into any car dealership, and the salesperson will immediately ask you one question: "What monthly payment are you looking for?"

This is a trap. The dealership knows something that most consumers forget in the heat of the moment: when you are entirely focused on the monthly payment, you lose sight of the total cost. By design, a car lease will almost always have a lower monthly payment than a standard 60-month auto loan for the exact same car.

Because the payment is lower, leasing feels like a "deal." But what are you actually trading away in exchange for that lower payment? This guide pulls back the curtain on how lease math is calculated, compares a $35,000 lease head-to-head with a loan, and reveals the hidden fees embedded in the fine print.

What Leasing Actually Means

When you buy a car with an auto loan, your payments are buying equity. When you make your final payment, the bank sends you the title, and the car is 100% yours.

When you lease a car, you are not buying a car—you are renting the depreciation.

A new car loses value the second you drive it off the lot. If a car costs $35,000 today, the dealership estimates it will only be worth $22,000 three years from now. That $22,000 figure is called the Residual Value. When you lease, you are simply paying the dealership for the $13,000 in value that the car loses over those three years, plus a financing fee.

At the end of month 36, you hand the keys back. You own absolutely nothing, you have no equity to trade in, and you have to start the cycle all over again.

The Math Behind the Payments

Car loans are easy to calculate using standard loan amortization. Lease calculations are intentionally complex to prevent consumers from easily comparing rates.

How a Lease is Calculated

A lease payment is made of two parts: the Depreciation Charge (the value lost) and the Finance Charge (the interest). Dealerships hide the interest rate by expressing it as a "Money Factor." To find the true APR, you must multiply the Money Factor by 2,400.

Monthly Lease Payment = Depreciation + Finance Charge Depreciation = (Capitalized Cost − Residual Value) ÷ Lease Term Finance Charge = (Capitalized Cost + Residual Value) × Money Factor *Money Factor = APR ÷ 2400
Real Example: $35,000 Car on a 36-Month Lease
• Cap Cost (MSRP): $35,000
• Residual Value (Value after 3 years): $22,050
• APR: 6% (Money factor = 0.0025)

Depreciation: ($35,000 - $22,050) ÷ 36 = $359.72
Finance Charge: ($35,000 + $22,050) × 0.0025 = $142.63
Monthly Lease Payment: $359.72 + $142.63 = $502/month

How an Auto Loan is Calculated

If we take that exact same $35,000 car and buy it using a standard 60-month loan at 6.5% APR (with a 10% down payment of $3,500), the formula yields a payment of $616/month.

Looking purely at cash flow, the lease saves you $114 a month. But what happens over time?

At the 3-year mark, buying has mathematically beaten leasing. The buyer has paid more cash out of pocket, but because they can sell the car and pay off the remaining loan, they actually walk away with positive equity in their pocket.

The 5-Year Picture (The "Perpetual Payment" Trap)

This is where leasing destroys long-term wealth. At Month 60, the buyer makes their final payment. They now own a $17,000 asset free and clear, and they have zero monthly car payments moving forward.

The lessee, however, had to return their car at Month 36 and sign a brand new 3-year lease. The lessee will continue paying $500+ a month, forever, in a perpetual cycle of renting. Over 10 years, the buyer will save tens of thousands of dollars simply by holding onto their paid-off vehicle.

When to Lease vs. Buy

Despite the math favoring buying in the long run, leasing is a highly valuable tool for certain lifestyles.

✅ Leasing Makes Sense If:

  • You absolutely require a brand-new car every 3 years for prestige or safety features.
  • You use the car for a business (lease payments can often be written off as a business expense).
  • You drive very predictable, low mileage (under 12,000 miles a year).
  • You want the peace of mind of a bumper-to-bumper warranty that never expires.

✅ Buying Makes Sense If:

  • You plan to keep the car for 5 to 10+ years.
  • You drive more than 15,000 miles a year.
  • You want the financial freedom of eventually having no monthly car payment.
  • You want to customize, modify, or ride with messy pets without fear of return penalties.

The Hidden Costs of Leasing

If you choose to lease, you must budget for the fees hidden in the fine print:

  • Acquisition Fee: A $500 to $1,000 fee charged just for initiating the lease. It is often rolled into the capitalized cost.
  • Disposition Fee: A $300 to $500 fee charged when you hand the keys back at the end of the lease to cover the dealer's cost of prepping it for resale.
  • The Mileage Penalty: Leases usually cap at 10k or 12k miles a year. If you go over, you will be charged $0.15 to $0.30 per mile. If you drive 5,000 miles over your limit, you will owe a surprise $1,250 bill when you return the car.
  • Excess Wear and Tear: If your kids spill juice on the seats, or you scrape the alloy wheels on a curb, the dealership will charge you repair fees at the end of the lease.

The Lease Buyout Option

What happens if you lease a car, fall in love with it, and want to keep it after three years? You can execute a Lease Buyout.

Your original contract states exactly what the car's "Residual Value" will be at the end of the lease. You have the right to purchase the car from the dealership for that exact price. This can sometimes be a massive financial win for you.

For example, if your contract states the residual value is $20,000, but due to a crazy used-car market, the car is actually worth $26,000 on Kelley Blue Book, you can buy the car for $20,000 and immediately have $6,000 in instant equity. (Conversely, if the car is only worth $15,000, you simply hand the keys back and let the bank eat the $5,000 loss).

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it financially better to lease or buy a car?
From a pure net-worth perspective, buying is mathematically superior over a 5-to-10-year horizon. Once a car loan is paid off, you own a depreciating—but valuable—asset with zero monthly payments. Leasing ensures you have a permanent monthly bill.
What is a "Money Factor" in leasing?
The Money Factor is how dealerships express the interest rate on a lease. To find out what interest rate they are actually charging you, multiply the money factor by 2,400. For example, a money factor of 0.0030 is equal to a 7.2% APR (0.0030 × 2400).
What happens if I crash a leased car?
If you total a leased car, your auto insurance will pay the bank the current market value of the car. If you owe more on the lease than the car is worth, you are liable for the difference. This is why almost all leases require you to carry Gap Insurance to cover that deficit.
Can I negotiate a lease price?
Yes! You can and should negotiate the "Capitalized Cost" (the purchase price of the vehicle) just as if you were buying it. You can also negotiate the Money Factor. The only number that is strictly non-negotiable is the Residual Value, which is set by the manufacturer's bank.
Can I end my lease early?
Yes, but it is incredibly expensive. You will generally have to pay an early termination fee, plus the remaining balance of your lease payments. A better option is to use a lease-swapping service where someone else takes over your contract.
Should I put money down on a lease?
