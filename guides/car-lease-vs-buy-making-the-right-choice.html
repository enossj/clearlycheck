Car Lease vs. Buy: Making the Right Choice

The lease payment is lower — but what does it actually cost over three years? We run the full numbers on a $35,000 car so you can see the real trade-off between leasing and buying.

Built & reviewed by Enos, ClearlyCheck · Last updated August 31, 2026 · 8 min read

The car dealership knows something most buyers don't: when you're focused on the monthly payment, you're not focused on the total cost. A lease will almost always have a lower monthly payment than a loan for the same car. But that's not the whole picture — and understanding what leasing actually means financially changes how you evaluate the decision.

This guide explains exactly how lease payments are calculated, runs a side-by-side comparison on a $35,000 vehicle, and gives you a clear framework for which option makes sense for your situation.

What Leasing Actually Means

When you lease a car, you're not buying it — you're renting the portion of its value that depreciates over the lease term. You pay for the difference between what the car is worth today and what it will be worth when you return it (its residual value), plus a financing charge on the full value of the car.

At the end of a lease, you hand the car back (or pay to buy it at the residual value). You own nothing and start over. That's the fundamental trade-off: lower monthly payments in exchange for no equity built, ever.

How a Lease Payment Is Calculated

Unlike a car loan, lease payments are not widely understood. Here's the actual formula:

Monthly Lease Payment = Depreciation Charge + Finance Charge Depreciation Charge = (Capitalised Cost − Residual Value) ÷ Lease Term (months) Finance Charge = (Capitalised Cost + Residual Value) × Money Factor Where Money Factor = APR ÷ 2400 (convert APR to monthly lease rate)
Lease Calculation — $35,000 Car, 36 months

Capitalised cost (MSRP, no down payment): $35,000

Residual value (63% of MSRP after 3 years): $22,050

APR: 6% → Money factor = 6 ÷ 2,400 = 0.0025

Depreciation charge: ($35,000 − $22,050) ÷ 36 = $12,950 ÷ 36 = $359.72/month

Finance charge: ($35,000 + $22,050) × 0.0025 = $57,050 × 0.0025 = $142.63/month

Base monthly payment: $359.72 + $142.63 = ~$502/month (before tax)

How a Car Loan Payment Is Calculated

A car loan uses the standard amortization formula. You finance the purchase price minus any down payment and pay it off over the loan term.

Loan Calculation — $35,000 Car, $3,500 down, 60 months at 6.5% APR

Loan amount: $35,000 − $3,500 = $31,500

Monthly rate: 6.5% ÷ 12 = 0.5417%

Formula: M = P × r(1+r)ⁿ / ((1+r)ⁿ − 1)

M = $31,500 × 0.005417 × (1.005417)⁶⁰ / ((1.005417)⁶⁰ − 1)

M = $31,500 × 0.005417 × 1.3831 / 0.3831 = ~$614/month

Total paid (loan): $3,500 + (60 × $614) = $3,500 + $36,840 = $40,340

Total interest paid: $40,340 − $35,000 = $5,340

The 3-Year Picture

After 3 years, the lease has cost you $18,072 net with nothing to show. Buying has cost you $3,554 net — but you own a $22,050 car and have 24 payments remaining. Leasing is not "cheaper." It's lower monthly payments in exchange for zero equity accumulation.

The owned car's ongoing cost drops to zero after the loan is paid, while the lease perpetually requires a monthly payment. Every additional year of ownership after loan payoff makes buying look better.

When Leasing Actually Makes Sense

Despite the math above, leasing is the right choice for some people in specific situations:

✅ Lease makes sense if you…

  • Prefer a new car every 2–3 years
  • Drive limited, predictable miles (under 12,000–15,000/year)
  • Always keep the car under warranty
  • Use the car for business (lease payments are often fully deductible)
  • Value low monthly cash flow commitment
  • Won't stress about wear-and-tear or mileage overages

✅ Buy makes sense if you…

  • Drive 15,000+ miles per year
  • Plan to keep the car 5+ years
  • Want eventual zero-payment ownership
  • Customise your vehicle (not allowed in leases)
  • Have variable annual mileage (trips, life changes)
  • Are building long-term net worth

The Hidden Costs of Leasing

The advertised lease payment is rarely your full cost:

  • Acquisition fee: $500–$1,000 due at signing, often buried in the capitalised cost
  • Disposition fee: $300–$500 at lease end if you don't buy the car
  • Excess mileage charges: $0.15–$0.30 per mile over your allotted annual miles — 5,000 extra miles at $0.25 = $1,250
  • Excess wear and tear: Dings, scratches, and worn tires above lease-defined standards result in charges at return
  • Gap insurance: Required by most lessors; covers the difference between the car's value and what you owe if it's totalled

Frequently Asked Questions

Financially, buying is better for most people in most situations — especially if you keep the car beyond the loan payoff. Once paid off, an owned car has no monthly payment. Leasing has lower monthly payments but you own nothing and always have a payment. Leasing makes sense if you drive limited miles, want new technology every few years, or use the car for business where payments are deductible.
A lease payment = depreciation charge + finance charge. The depreciation charge is (cap cost − residual value) ÷ months. The finance charge is (cap cost + residual value) × money factor. The money factor is the interest rate expressed as APR ÷ 2400. A lower residual value or higher money factor increases the monthly payment significantly.
Excess mileage is charged at return, typically $0.15–$0.30 per mile over the contracted limit. If your lease allows 10,000 miles/year and you drive 14,000, you owe overage charges for 4,000 miles × the per-mile rate — potentially $600–$1,200 at lease end. If you expect to exceed the limit, negotiate more miles upfront (cheaper) or choose to buy instead.
The EPA considers 40+ MPG combined to be fuel-efficient for gasoline vehicles. The average new car achieves 28–30 MPG. At 15,000 miles/year and $3.50/gallon, moving from 20 MPG to 35 MPG saves about $1,125 per year in fuel.
