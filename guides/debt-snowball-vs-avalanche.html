Debt Snowball vs. Avalanche: Which Pays Off Your Debt Faster?

Two strategies, one math problem. We run a real three-debt example through both methods — and the results might surprise you.

Built & reviewed by Enos, ClearlyCheck · Last updated August 31, 2026 · 7 min read

If you've searched for how to pay off debt faster, you've almost certainly encountered two competing schools of thought: the debt snowball and the debt avalanche. Financial experts argue passionately about which is better. The answer, as with most personal finance questions, is: it depends — and the difference is more nuanced than most articles admit.

This guide runs both strategies through the same real numbers so you can see exactly what each one costs and how long each one takes.

The Setup: What Both Methods Have in Common

Regardless of which strategy you use, the mechanics are identical:

  1. Make minimum payments on every debt every month — no exceptions. Missing minimums costs you fees and damages your credit.
  2. Direct any extra money you can afford toward one target debt.
  3. Once the target debt is paid off, roll its full payment (minimum + extra) into the next target. This is what creates the "snowball" or "avalanche" momentum.

The only difference between the two methods is which debt you target first.

The Worked Example: Three Real Debts

Let's say you have three debts and can afford an extra $250/month beyond your minimum payments:

Your Debt Picture

Debt A (Personal Loan): $2,100 balance · 11% APR · $42/month minimum

Debt B (Credit Card): $8,500 balance · 18% APR · $170/month minimum

Debt C (Credit Card): $4,900 balance · 24% APR · $98/month minimum

Total minimums: $310/month · Extra available: $250/month · Total monthly budget: $560/month

Strategy 1: The Debt Avalanche

The avalanche method targets your highest interest rate debt first, regardless of balance size. The logic is purely mathematical: a high-interest debt is the most expensive debt you own, so eliminating it first minimises the total interest you pay.

Avalanche order: Debt C (24% APR) → Debt B (18% APR) → Debt A (11% APR)

Avalanche — Phase 1: Attack Debt C ($4,900 at 24%)

Monthly payment on C: $98 minimum + $250 extra = $348/month

Monthly interest on C at start: $4,900 × (24%/12) = $98.00

Net principal paid each month (Month 1): $348 − $98 = $250

Debt C is cleared in approximately 17 months, paying roughly $790 in interest on this debt.

Avalanche — Phase 2: Roll into Debt B ($8,500 at 18%)

Monthly payment on B: $170 minimum + $348 rolled over = $518/month

Debt B is cleared in approximately 20 months, paying roughly $1,230 in interest on this debt.

Avalanche — Phase 3: Roll into Debt A ($2,100 at 11%)

Monthly payment on A: $42 minimum + $518 rolled over = $560/month

Debt A clears in 4 months, paying roughly $45 in interest on this debt.

Avalanche Result

Total time: ~41 months (3 years, 5 months)  |  Total interest paid: ~$2,065

Strategy 2: The Debt Snowball

The snowball method targets your smallest balance first, regardless of interest rate. Dave Ramsey popularised this approach because the psychology matters: every debt you eliminate in full is a concrete win that makes you more likely to stay committed to the plan.

Snowball order: Debt A (smallest: $2,100) → Debt C ($4,900) → Debt B (largest: $8,500)

Snowball — Phase 1: Attack Debt A ($2,100 at 11%)

Monthly payment on A: $42 minimum + $250 extra = $292/month

Monthly interest on A at start: $2,100 × (11%/12) = $19.25

Net principal paid each month (Month 1): $292 − $19.25 = $272.75

Debt A is cleared in approximately 8 months, paying roughly $80 in interest.

✓ First debt eliminated. You're already debt-free on one account.

Snowball — Phase 2: Roll into Debt C ($4,900 at 24%)

Monthly payment on C: $98 minimum + $292 rolled over = $390/month

Debt C is cleared in approximately 15 months, paying roughly $870 in interest.

✓ Second debt eliminated. Two down, one to go.

Snowball — Phase 3: Roll into Debt B ($8,500 at 18%)

Monthly payment on B: $170 minimum + $390 rolled over = $560/month

Debt B is cleared in approximately 20 months, paying roughly $1,545 in interest.

Snowball Result

Total time: ~43 months (3 years, 7 months)  |  Total interest paid: ~$2,495

Which Strategy Should You Choose?

The honest answer: the one you will actually stick to. A mathematically perfect plan that you abandon in month five is worse than an imperfect plan you follow for three years.

Choose Avalanche if…

You're motivated by numbers

You find it easy to stay disciplined, you're comfortable not seeing individual debt balances hit zero quickly, and you want to minimise the total cost of your debt repayment.

Saves ~$430 in this example
Choose Snowball if…

You need wins to stay motivated

You've tried paying off debt before and given up. You know you need to see quick progress. You're more likely to stay consistent if you can cross individual debts off your list.

Eliminates first debt 9 months sooner

The Hybrid Approach: Best of Both Worlds

Many financial advisers recommend a pragmatic middle ground: start with one small quick-win debt to build momentum (snowball), then switch to attacking by interest rate (avalanche) for the remaining debts. In our example, that would mean:

  • Clear Debt A ($2,100) in 8 months — quick win, low cost ($80 interest)
  • Switch to Debt C ($4,900 at 24%) next — highest remaining rate
  • Finish with Debt B ($8,500 at 18%)

This hybrid path is mathematically near-identical to the pure avalanche (since Debt A was already low-rate) but gives you a concrete victory in month eight to keep you going.

One Change That Beats Both Strategies

Whichever method you use, increasing your monthly payment is more impactful than the choice of strategy. In our example, adding just $50 more per month (bringing the budget from $560 to $610) shaves roughly four months off the total timeline and saves over $250 in interest — more than the entire difference between the two strategies.
