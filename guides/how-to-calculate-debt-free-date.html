How to Calculate Your Debt-Free Date (The Formula, Explained)

Three inputs. One formula. Your exact payoff date. We walk through the amortization math step by step — and show what adding $100/month really saves.

Built & reviewed by Enos, ClearlyCheck · Last updated August 31, 2026 · 8 min read

Most people know roughly how much debt they have. Very few know exactly when they'll be free of it — or what it's actually going to cost them in total. The answer lives inside a formula that looks intimidating but is really just arithmetic once you understand what each piece means.

This guide walks through the loan amortization formula step by step on a real $12,000 example, shows you the early months of the payment schedule in detail, and demonstrates exactly how adding $100/month to your payment changes everything. By the end, you'll understand precisely how the math works — and have a link to do it in two seconds instead of twenty minutes.

The Three Numbers You Need

To calculate your debt-free date, you only need three pieces of information:

  1. P — Your current balance (the amount you owe right now)
  2. r — Your monthly interest rate (your annual APR divided by 12)
  3. M — Your monthly payment (the fixed amount you pay each month)

One critical constraint: your monthly payment must be greater than the monthly interest charge. If it isn't, your balance grows every month and you'll never pay it off. We'll come back to this.

The Formula

The standard loan amortization formula calculates n, the number of months until the balance reaches zero:

n = −ln(1 − (r × P) / M) / ln(1 + r) Where: P = current balance r = monthly interest rate (APR ÷ 12, as a decimal) M = monthly payment n = number of months to payoff ln = natural logarithm

If the term "natural logarithm" makes you want to close the tab — don't. You don't need to calculate it by hand. We'll walk through the numbers on a real example, and then show you how to get the answer in two seconds using our calculator.

Step-by-Step: The $12,000 Example

Let's say you have a personal loan or credit card with these details:

The Scenario

Balance (P): $12,000

Annual interest rate (APR): 19%

Monthly payment (M): $300

Convert APR to monthly rate

Divide the annual rate by 12 to get r: 19% ÷ 12 = 1.5833% per month = 0.015833 as a decimal.

Check that the payment covers the interest

Monthly interest in Month 1: $12,000 × 0.015833 = $190.00. Your $300 payment covers this with $110 left over to reduce the balance. Good — you'll make progress every month.

Plug into the formula

n = −ln(1 − (0.015833 × 12,000) / 300) / ln(1 + 0.015833)

n = −ln(1 − 190.00 / 300) / ln(1.015833)

n = −ln(1 − 0.6333) / ln(1.015833)

n = −ln(0.3667) / 0.015709

n = −(−1.0030) / 0.015709

n = 1.0030 / 0.015709 ≈ 63.9 months → round up to 64 months

Convert months to years and months

64 months = 5 years and 4 months. If you start paying today (September 2026), your debt-free date is approximately January 2032.

Calculate total interest paid

Total paid = monthly payment × number of months = $300 × 64 = $19,200. Total interest = $19,200 − $12,000 = $7,200 in interest on top of your $12,000 balance.

Result: $300/month payment

Payoff: 64 months (5 years, 4 months) · Total interest: $7,200 · Total paid: $19,200

How the Balance Actually Drops Month by Month

It's not just about the end date — understanding the month-by-month pattern explains why your balance feels slow to move early on. Here's what the first six months of payments look like:

Month Payment Interest Portion Principal Portion Remaining Balance
1 $300 $190.00 $110.00 $11,890.00
2 $300 $188.26 $111.74 $11,778.26
3 $300 $186.49 $113.51 $11,664.75
6 $300 $181.24 $118.76 $11,326.83
12 $300 $171.42 $128.58 $10,678.57
24 $300 $148.60 $151.40 $9,182.29
48 $300 $84.90 $215.10 $5,142.31
64 (final) ~$299 ~$4.72 ~$294 $0

Notice how in Month 1, 63% of your payment goes to interest ($190 out of $300). By Month 48, the split has flipped — now 72% goes to principal. This is why the final months of any loan feel so much faster: each payment makes a much bigger dent in the balance.

What Adding $100/Month Does

Now let's run the same scenario but with $400/month instead of $300. Same formula, same balance, same rate — just one number changes.

Same Loan, $400/Month Payment

r = 0.015833, P = $12,000, M = $400

n = −ln(1 − (0.015833 × 12,000) / 400) / ln(1.015833)

n = −ln(1 − 190.00 / 400) / ln(1.015833)

n = −ln(1 − 0.475) / 0.015709

n = −ln(0.525) / 0.015709

n = −(−0.6444) / 0.015709

n = 0.6444 / 0.015709 ≈ 41.0 months → 41 months

Result: $400/month payment

Payoff: 41 months (3 years, 5 months) · Total interest: $4,400 · Total paid: $16,400

Adding just $100 to your monthly payment — from $300 to $400 — cuts 23 months off your timeline and saves $2,800 in interest. That $2,800 is money that would otherwise go to the lender instead of staying in your pocket.

What If My Payment Barely Covers the Interest?

If your monthly interest charge equals or exceeds your payment, the formula breaks down — and so does your debt payoff. On a $12,000 balance at 19% APR, the minimum payment needed just to stop the balance from growing is $190.01/month. Anything less and the debt grows indefinitely.

This is the danger zone for high-APR credit cards with very low minimums. If you find yourself in this situation, the most urgent move is to find any way to increase your payment above the monthly interest charge — even $20 more per month gives you forward momentum.
