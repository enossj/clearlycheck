How to Calculate Your Paycheck: The Complete Guide

You accepted the job offer. The salary looked great. And then your first paycheck arrived, and the number was way smaller than you expected. Sound familiar? You're not alone. Millions of Americans are surprised by the gap between what they earn on paper and what actually hits their bank account.

This guide breaks down exactly how your paycheck is calculated, what each deduction means, and how to estimate your take-home pay before you even start a new job. No jargon, no filler — just the straight facts.

Gross Pay vs. Net Pay: What's the Difference?

Let's start with the basics, because this is where most of the confusion lives:

  • Gross pay is the total amount your employer owes you before any deductions. If your offer letter says $60,000 per year, that's your gross annual salary. If you're paid $25/hour, your gross pay for a 40-hour week is $1,000.
  • Net pay (also called "take-home pay") is what you actually receive after taxes and deductions are subtracted. This is the number that shows up in your bank account on payday.

The difference between these two numbers can be substantial. For most workers, 20% to 30% of gross pay goes to taxes and mandatory deductions, sometimes more depending on your state and income level.

Understanding FICA Taxes (Social Security & Medicare)

FICA is probably the most misunderstood deduction on your pay stub. It stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act, and it funds two programs:

  • Social Security (6.2%): This tax applies to every dollar you earn up to the annual wage base limit ($168,600 in 2024, adjusted annually for inflation). Once your earnings pass that threshold, you stop paying the Social Security portion for the rest of the year.
  • Medicare (1.45%): This tax applies to all of your earnings with no cap. If you earn above $200,000 ($250,000 for married filing jointly), an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax kicks in.

Combined, the standard employee FICA rate is 7.65%. Your employer pays a matching 7.65% on top of that, but only the employee portion appears on your pay stub.

Quick math: On a $60,000 salary, FICA alone takes $4,590 per year — that's $176.54 per bi-weekly paycheck gone before federal or state taxes even touch your income.

Why FICA Matters More Than You Think

Unlike federal income tax, FICA is a flat-rate tax. There are no brackets, no deductions, no credits to reduce it. Whether you earn $30,000 or $150,000, you pay 7.65% from dollar one. That makes it the single largest payroll deduction for most workers earning under $50,000.

Federal Income Tax Brackets (2024)

The United States uses a progressive tax system. That means you don't pay one flat rate on all your income. Instead, your income is split into chunks, and each chunk is taxed at a progressively higher rate.

Here's the current bracket structure for a single filer:

Taxable Income Tax Rate
$0 – $11,600 10%
$11,601 – $47,150 12%
$47,151 – $100,525 22%
$100,526 – $191,950 24%
$191,951 – $243,725 32%
$243,726 – $609,350 35%
$609,351+ 37%

Marginal vs. Effective Tax Rate

This is a point that trips people up all the time. If you earn $60,000, you do not pay 22% on the entire $60,000. Here's how it actually works:

  • The first $11,600 is taxed at 10% = $1,160
  • The next $35,550 ($11,601 – $47,150) is taxed at 12% = $4,266
  • The remaining $12,850 ($47,151 – $60,000) is taxed at 22% = $2,827

Total federal tax: $8,253, which works out to an effective rate of about 13.8% — not 22%. Your marginal rate (the rate on your last dollar) is 22%, but your effective rate is always lower because of how the brackets stack.

In the calculator above, we use a single estimated federal rate for simplicity. If you know your effective federal rate, plug it in directly for a more accurate result.

State Income Taxes: The Wild Card

State taxes vary dramatically across the country. This single variable can make a huge difference in your take-home pay.

States With No Income Tax

Nine states don't tax your wages at all:

  • Alaska
  • Florida
  • Nevada
  • New Hampshire (taxes only interest and dividends)
  • South Dakota
  • Tennessee
  • Texas
  • Washington
  • Wyoming

If you live in one of these states, set the state tax rate to 0% in the calculator above.

States With the Highest Income Taxes

On the other end of the spectrum, some states take a significant cut:

  • California: up to 13.3% (highest top marginal rate in the US)
  • Hawaii: up to 11%
  • New Jersey: up to 10.75%
  • Oregon: up to 9.9%
  • Minnesota: up to 9.85%
  • New York: up to 10.9% (plus NYC local tax of up to 3.876%)

Most states fall somewhere in the 3% to 6% range. For a rough estimate, 4% to 5% is a reasonable default if you're not sure of your exact state rate.

Pay Frequency: How It Affects Your Paycheck

Your pay frequency doesn't change how much you earn per year, but it does change the size of each individual paycheck. Here's how the same $60,000 salary looks across different schedules:

Pay Frequency Checks/Year Gross Per Check
Weekly 52 $1,153.85
Bi-weekly 26 $2,307.69
Semi-monthly 24 $2,500.00
Monthly 12 $5,000.00

Bi-weekly is the most common pay schedule in the US. One bonus: in two months each year, you'll receive three paychecks instead of two, which can feel like a nice financial boost (even though the annual total is the same).

How to Read Your Pay Stub

Your pay stub is the receipt for your paycheck. Whether it's paper or digital, it contains a wealth of information. Here's what each section typically includes:

Earnings Section

  • Regular hours/salary: Your base pay for the period
  • Overtime: Hours worked beyond 40/week, typically paid at 1.5x your regular rate
  • Bonuses or commissions: Additional compensation earned during the period
  • Year-to-date (YTD) totals: Cumulative earnings from January 1 through the current pay date

Deductions Section

  • Federal income tax: Based on your W-4 withholding elections
  • State income tax: Varies by state
  • Social Security (OASDI): The 6.2% portion of FICA
  • Medicare: The 1.45% portion of FICA
  • Health insurance: Your employee portion of medical, dental, or vision premiums
  • 401(k) or retirement contributions: Pre-tax or Roth contributions
  • HSA/FSA contributions: Pre-tax health savings or flexible spending
  • Local taxes: City or county taxes (applies in some jurisdictions like NYC, Philadelphia, and various Ohio cities)

Net Pay Section

This is the bottom line — the amount deposited into your bank account. It's your gross pay minus every deduction listed above. If the number feels shockingly small compared to your salary, now you know exactly where the money went.

Pre-Tax Deductions: How to Keep More of Your Paycheck

Pre-tax deductions are your best tool for reducing your tax burden legally. These amounts are subtracted from your gross pay before taxes are calculated, which lowers your taxable income.

Common Pre-Tax Deductions

  • Traditional 401(k) contributions: Up to $23,000 in 2024 ($30,500 if you're 50 or older). Every dollar you contribute reduces your taxable income dollar-for-dollar.
  • Health insurance premiums: Most employer-sponsored plans use pre-tax payroll deductions.
  • Health Savings Account (HSA): If you have a high-deductible health plan, you can contribute up to $4,150 (individual) or $8,300 (family) pre-tax in 2024.
  • Flexible Spending Account (FSA): Up to $3,200 in 2024 for healthcare expenses, deducted pre-tax.
  • Commuter benefits: Up to $315/month pre-tax for transit or parking.

Example: If you earn $60,000 and contribute $6,000 to your 401(k), your taxable income drops to $54,000. At a 22% marginal rate, that saves you $1,320 in federal taxes alone — meaning your $6,000 contribution effectively only "costs" you about $4,680 in reduced take-home pay.

Salary vs. Hourly: How It Affects Your Paycheck

Whether you're salaried or hourly changes how your gross pay is calculated, but the tax deductions work the same way.

  • Salaried employees: Your gross pay per period is simply your annual salary divided by the number of pay periods. It's the same every paycheck (assuming no overtime or bonuses).
  • Hourly employees: Your gross pay per period is your hourly rate × hours worked. This can fluctuate week to week. For a standard 40-hour week, annual gross = hourly rate × 40 × 52.

Real-World Paycheck Example: $60,000 Salary

Let's walk through a concrete example so you can see exactly how the math works. Assumptions: single filer, bi-weekly pay, 12% effective federal rate, 4% state tax rate, no pre-tax deductions.

  1. Gross pay per paycheck: $60,000 ÷ 26 = $2,307.69
  2. FICA (7.65%): $2,307.69 × 0.0765 = −$176.54
  3. Federal tax (12%): $2,307.69 × 0.12 = −$276.92
  4. State tax (4%): $2,307.69 × 0.04 = −$92.31
  5. Net take-home pay: $2,307.69 − $176.54 − $276.92 − $92.31 = $1,761.92

Over a full year, that's $45,809.92 in actual take-home pay — meaning $14,190.08 (23.65%) of your salary went to taxes. And that's before any health insurance, retirement contributions, or local taxes.

Common Paycheck Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing gross for net: When budgeting, always use your net pay. A $60,000 salary does not give you $5,000/month to spend.
  • Ignoring state taxes: If you're moving to a new state, factor in the tax difference. Going from Texas (0%) to California (9%+) on a $70,000 salary costs you $6,300+ per year.
  • Not adjusting your W-4: If you're getting a huge tax refund every year, you're over-withholding. That's essentially giving the IRS an interest-free loan. Adjust your W-4 to take home more each paycheck.
  • Forgetting about FICA: When people say "my tax rate is 12%," they usually forget the 7.65% FICA on top of that. Your actual combined tax burden is closer to 24%.
  • Skipping retirement contributions: A 401(k) match is free money. If your employer matches up to 4%, contributing less than 4% means you're leaving compensation on the table.

Tips for Maximizing Your Take-Home Pay

  • Max out pre-tax deductions. Every dollar in your 401(k) or HSA reduces your taxable income and shrinks your tax bill.
  • Claim all eligible tax credits. Credits like the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC), Child Tax Credit, and Saver's Credit directly reduce your tax liability.
  • Adjust your W-4 withholdings. Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator to fine-tune your withholdings so you're not over- or under-paying throughout the year.
  • Consider your state carefully. If you have location flexibility (remote work, for example), living in a no-income-tax state can save you thousands annually.
  • Use a Roth 401(k) strategically. If you're in a low bracket now but expect higher income later, Roth contributions (taxed now, tax-free later) can save you money long-term — though they won't reduce your current tax bill.
