What this calculator does

A time card (or timesheet) calculator turns your clock-in and clock-out times into paid hours. For each day it works out the length of your shift, subtracts any unpaid break, and adds the days together into a weekly total. If you enter an hourly rate it also estimates your gross pay, and it can split your hours into regular and overtime once you pass 40 in a week.

How the hours are worked out

The maths for each day is simple:

  • Shift length = clock-out time − clock-in time
  • Paid hours = shift length − unpaid break

If your clock-out time is earlier than your clock-in time, the calculator assumes the shift crossed midnight and adds 24 hours — so an 8:00 PM to 4:00 AM shift correctly counts as 8 hours, not a negative number.

How overtime works

Under the US Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), most hourly employees must be paid 1.5× their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. With the overtime option on, this tool caps regular hours at 40 and pays the rest at time-and-a-half. For example, 45 hours at $20/hour is 40 × $20 + 5 × $30 = $950. A few states (such as California) also require daily overtime after 8 hours — this calculator uses the federal weekly rule, so check your state if daily overtime applies to you.

Paid vs. unpaid breaks

Only enter unpaid break minutes. Under federal rules, short rest breaks (usually under 20 minutes) are typically paid and shouldn't be deducted, while genuine meal breaks (usually 30 minutes or more, free of duties) are typically unpaid. If a break is paid, leave it out so your total isn't reduced.

The 7-minute rounding rule

Some employers round clock times to the nearest quarter-hour. The FLSA allows this as long as it's neutral over time: minutes 1-7 past a quarter-hour round down, and 8-14 round up. Rounding must never systematically favour the employer. This calculator uses your exact times; if your workplace rounds, expect small differences from your official pay stub.

Sources & method

Hour totals use the clock-in/clock-out method described above; overtime follows the US Department of Labor FLSA standard of 1.5× the regular rate beyond 40 hours per workweek (dol.gov). Break and rounding guidance also follows federal FLSA rules. State laws — daily overtime, mandatory break pay, and rounding limits — can differ, and this tool estimates gross pay only (before taxes and deductions). For payroll disputes or exact figures, rely on your employer's official records or a qualified professional. This tool is for general guidance, not legal or tax advice.
