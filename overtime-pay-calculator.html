How overtime pay is calculated

Under the US Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), non-exempt employees must be paid at least 1.5 times their regular hourly rate for every hour worked beyond 40 in a workweek. The math is simple once you split your hours:

  1. Hours up to 40 are paid at your normal rate — that's your regular pay.
  2. Hours over 40 are paid at your normal rate × 1.5 — that's your overtime pay.
  3. Add them together for your gross pay that week.

Example: at $20/hour working 47 hours — 40 × $20 = $800 regular, plus 7 × $30 = $210 overtime, for $1,010 gross. This calculator does that split automatically and lets you change the 40-hour threshold or the 1.5× multiplier if your job or state uses different rules.

Time and a half vs. double time

Time and a half (1.5×) is the federal overtime minimum. Double time (2×) is not required by federal law — it's an employer policy or a state rule. California, for instance, requires double time after 12 hours in a single day. If your employer pays double time for holidays or long shifts, set the multiplier to 2× above.

Is overtime taxed more?

This is the most common overtime myth. Overtime is taxed at the same rates as your regular pay — there is no special "overtime tax." What people notice is withholding: a bigger paycheck can look like it belongs to a higher-earning person, so payroll withholds more for that one check. Your actual tax is settled on your total annual income, and any extra withheld comes back at tax time. Every overtime dollar is worth just as much as a regular dollar.

Who qualifies for overtime

Most hourly workers are non-exempt and owed overtime. Some salaried employees in executive, administrative, professional, outside-sales, and certain computer roles are exempt if they meet federal salary and duties tests. States can be stricter than federal law — several require daily overtime — so the federal 40-hour rule is a floor, not a ceiling. If your situation is unusual, your state labor department is the authority.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Forgetting the workweek is fixed. Overtime is per workweek, not per pay period — working 30 hours one week and 50 the next still earns 10 hours of overtime, even if the biweekly total is 80.
  • Leaving out shift differentials or bonuses. Non-discretionary bonuses can raise your "regular rate" for overtime purposes. This tool uses the base rate you enter.
  • Assuming salary means no overtime. Being paid a salary does not automatically make you exempt — the duties and salary-level tests decide.

Sources & method

Overtime rules follow the US Department of Labor Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) standard: 1.5× the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. Calculations here use the rate, hours, threshold, and multiplier you enter: regular pay = (hours up to threshold) × rate; overtime pay = (hours over threshold) × rate × multiplier; effective rate = gross ÷ total hours. Period figures scale the weekly result (biweekly ×2, monthly ×52/12, annual ×52) assuming identical hours each week. This is a general estimate, not legal, tax, or payroll advice — state rules and individual circumstances vary, so confirm with your employer, payroll department, or your state labor office.
