How this mortgage calculator works

Your monthly mortgage payment is made up of several parts, often abbreviated PITI: Principal, Interest, Taxes, and Insurance. The principal and interest portion is calculated using the standard amortization formula, which spreads your loan amount across equal monthly payments over the loan term at your fixed interest rate. Property taxes, homeowners insurance, PMI (if applicable), and HOA fees are then added on top — these are typically collected monthly by your lender and paid out on your behalf when due, through what's called an escrow account.

What goes into your monthly payment

ComponentWhat it is
PrincipalThe portion of your payment that reduces your loan balance
InterestThe cost of borrowing, calculated on your remaining balance each month
Property taxSet by your local government, usually 0.5%–2.5% of home value per year
Home insuranceProtects the home against damage; often required by the lender
PMIRequired on most conventional loans when your down payment is under 20%
HOA feeA separate monthly fee for homes in a homeowners association — not part of your loan, but often bundled into total housing cost

Why early payments are mostly interest

In the early years of a mortgage, most of each payment goes toward interest rather than principal. That's because interest is calculated on your remaining balance, which is largest at the start of the loan. As the balance shrinks, less of each payment goes to interest and more goes to paying down principal — which is why the amortization schedule above shows the principal/interest split shifting steadily over time, even though your total payment stays the same.

Should you make extra payments?

Extra payments go straight to principal, which reduces the balance interest is calculated on for every remaining month of the loan. Because of how amortization compounds, even a modest extra payment early in the loan can save far more in total interest than the same amount paid later. The trade-offs to weigh:

  • Pros: Less total interest paid, a shorter loan term, and full ownership sooner.
  • Cons: Money put toward extra payments isn't available for other goals, and if your mortgage rate is low, investing that money elsewhere might outperform the interest saved.

Check with your lender before making extra payments — some loans carry prepayment penalties, and you'll want to confirm extra payments are applied to principal, not held as a credit toward your next regular payment.
