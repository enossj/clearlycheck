Why "one dog year = seven human years" is a myth

The classic rule of multiplying a pet's age by seven has been around for decades, but it was never based on real biology — it seems to have started as a rough marketing-era shorthand. Pets don't age at a steady rate. They grow up dramatically fast in their first two years, reaching the equivalent of a young adult, and then age more gradually. A one-year-old dog isn't a seven-year-old child; it's closer to a 15-year-old teenager, already able to reproduce.

How dogs actually age

Veterinary guidelines (and a 2019 epigenetic "clock" study of DNA methylation) describe a curve, not a straight line:

  • Year 1 ≈ 15 human years
  • Year 2 ≈ +9 more, reaching about 24
  • Each year after ≈ +4 to +7, depending on size

Size matters after year two. Small dogs live longer and age slowly, while large and giant breeds age faster and reach their senior years earlier. That's why this calculator asks for your dog's size — it changes the per-year rate.

How cats age

Cats follow a similar fast-then-slow curve, but aging is more consistent across breeds than in dogs. The first year is about 15 human years, the second reaches roughly 24, and each year after adds about 4. So a 10-year-old cat is around 56 in human terms. Indoor cats commonly live into their late teens, which is the equivalent of a human in their 80s or 90s.

Life stages

Human-age equivalents are a fun shorthand, but vets increasingly talk in life stages — puppy/kitten, young adult, mature adult, and senior — because care needs (diet, checkup frequency, screening) change with each. Large dogs may be considered senior by age six, while a small dog or cat might not be senior until ten or later.

Sources & method

This calculator uses the widely published veterinary approximation — about 15 human years for the first year, 24 by the second, and a size-based increment of roughly 4 (small) to 7 (giant) human years per year after that — consistent with guidance from the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA), the American Animal Hospital Association (AAHA) life-stage guidelines, and the American Kennel Club. The size-based curve is also supported by a 2019 epigenetic aging study (Wang et al., Cell Systems). These are estimates; individual aging varies with breed, genetics, and health. For your pet's specific health and life stage, consult your veterinarian — this tool is not veterinary advice.
